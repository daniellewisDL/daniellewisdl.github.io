Why this guide exists

For most of the last decade, "responsible AI" meant a page of principles on a corporate website. That era is over. Responsible AI is now a control and assurance discipline. It has statutory deadlines, supervisory expectations, litigation risk and board accountability, and it needs the same evidence you would expect for credit risk, financial reporting or cyber security.

Three forces made the shift. First, hard law arrived. The EU AI Act is in force, and its obligations for high-risk systems such as creditworthiness assessment now apply from December 2027 under the 2026 Digital Omnibus. The Council of Europe's Framework Convention is the first binding international AI treaty. Data protection law already governs automated decision-making almost everywhere. Second, liability moved to deployers. Courts and regulators have held airlines, pharmacies, lenders and employers answerable for what their AI does, whoever built it. Third, generative AI made failure cheap and everyday. The typical AI incident today is not a rogue superintelligence. It is an unverified output, such as a fabricated citation, a wrong policy statement or an invented fact, that reaches a customer, a regulator or a court.

Principles have converged: almost every framework asks for fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, safety and human oversight. Practice has not. What separates organisations now is whether those words are wired into inventories, risk tiering, testing, sign-off, monitoring and escalation that actually bite, and whether someone independent can prove it. This guide is about that wiring.

The argument in six sentences

  1. Ethics supplies the “why”: consequentialist, duty-based and virtue lenses each catch failures the others miss, and a mature programme uses all three (Chapter 02).
  2. Risk supplies the “what”: bias, opacity, manipulation, safety, privacy, reputational and model risk are connected, so a failure in one spreads to the others (Chapter 03).
  3. Mathematics sets the limits: fairness cannot be fully automated, because when base rates differ the common fairness criteria are mutually incompatible. Choosing between them is a governance decision, not a technical one (Chapters 04–06).
  4. Law and standards set the floor: the EU AI Act, UK sector regulation, US model-risk guidance, NIST and ISO/IEC 42001 are converging on the same operational expectations (Chapter 07).
  5. Implementation and controls make it real: an operating model, a lifecycle with gates, and a library of preventive, detective and corrective controls, each of which can be tested (Chapters 08–10).
  6. Trust is the prize: in banking especially, trust is the balance sheet. Risk appetite, measurement and board reporting are how an institution shows it deserves that trust (Chapters 11–13).

Chapter 01

A Short History of Machine Ethics

Responsible AI did not begin with ChatGPT. Its ideas come from cybernetics, medical ethics, consumer-credit law and the model-risk failures of the 2008 crisis. Knowing that lineage explains why today's frameworks look the way they do, and why banks are better prepared than they think.

In brief

  • The core questions (who is accountable, can a decision be explained, is it fair) were first asked of credit scorecards in the 1970s, not of neural networks.
  • Five eras: early warnings (1940s–70s), the scorecard (1956–2007), model risk (2008–2015), algorithmic accountability (2016–2019), and principles to proof (2020–2026).
  • Each era left behind an institution we still use: adverse-action notices, model validation, fairness metrics, ethics principles, and now statutory conformity and assurance.
  • Medical ethics is the closest precedent: scandal, then codes, then independent review boards, then licensed professionals. AI is partway along the same path.

Era I — Early warnings (1942–1979)

The popular story begins with fiction. In 1942 Isaac Asimov's short story Runaround set out the "Three Laws of Robotics". The laws are a useful teaching device precisely because the stories show them failing: rules that look complete turn out to be ambiguous, conflicting and easy to game at the edges. The same problem later appears as specification gaming in machine learning.

The serious intellectual origin is Norbert Wiener, the founder of cybernetics. In Cybernetics (1948) and The Human Use of Human Beings (1950) Wiener argued that machines able to learn and make decisions would have social consequences their designers had a moral duty to anticipate. In 1960 he wrote that if we use a mechanical agency whose operation we cannot efficiently interfere with, we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose we really desire. That is the alignment problem, stated sixty years early.

Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" and the 1956 Dartmouth workshop that named "artificial intelligence" founded the field. In 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA, a simple pattern-matching "therapist", showed something that still matters for chatbot risk: people readily attribute understanding and empathy to software that has neither. Weizenbaum's Computer Power and Human Reason (1976) argued that some decisions should not be delegated to machines even if they could make them. The guide returns often to that idea, just because we can does not mean we should.

At the same time, and quite separately, medical ethics was building the institutions AI ethics now borrows. The Nuremberg Code (1947) established informed consent after the atrocities of Nazi medical experimentation. The Declaration of Helsinki (1964) extended it to clinical research. The US Belmont Report (1978–79), written in response to the Tuskegee syphilis study, distilled research ethics into three principles: respect for persons, beneficence and justice. Beauchamp and Childress's Principles of Biomedical Ethics (1979) added non-maleficence. Their four principles are the direct ancestors of the principle lists in almost every AI framework today.

Era II — The age of the scorecard (1956–2007)

For banks, automated decision-making is decades old. Fair, Isaac and Company was founded in 1956 and sold its first credit scoring system in 1958. By the 1970s statistical scorecards were replacing the branch manager's judgement across consumer credit. They raised exactly the questions we now ask of AI, and legislators answered them.

  • Fair Credit Reporting Act (US, 1970): rights to know what data is held about you and to dispute inaccuracies, the forerunner of data subject access rights.
  • Equal Credit Opportunity Act (US, 1974) and Regulation B: prohibited discrimination on protected characteristics and required lenders to give specific principal reasons for adverse action. This is the first legally mandated explainability requirement for an automated decision, and it still binds complex models today. The US CFPB reminded lenders in 2022 and 2023 that "the algorithm is too complex" is no defence.
  • UK Consumer Credit Act 1974 and Data Protection Act 1984: the UK's parallel foundations.
  • St George's Hospital Medical School (UK, 1988): the Commission for Racial Equality found that a computer program used to screen medical-school applicants had been built to replicate past human selection decisions, and so systematically marked down women and applicants with non-European names. It is arguably the first well-documented case of algorithmic discrimination, and its lesson, that a model trained on biased historical decisions automates the bias, is exactly the lesson of Amazon's recruiting tool thirty years later.
  • EU Data Protection Directive (1995), Article 15: a right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that significantly affect a person. It became Article 22 of the GDPR.
  • Basel II (2004): the internal ratings-based approach let banks use their own statistical models to set regulatory capital. Models became prudentially material, and supervisors began to care in detail about model design, data and validation.

Why this matters today

Banks already have fifty years of institutional muscle for governing automated decisions: adverse-action reasons, fair-lending testing, model validation, data-protection controls. The most effective responsible-AI programmes in financial services extend this machinery rather than build a parallel structure. Module 5 of the source material says it plainly: “Begin with existing model risk frameworks.”

Era III — Model risk becomes systemic (2007–2015)

The 2008 financial crisis was, among other things, a model failure. The Gaussian copula, which let structured-credit desks price the correlation of mortgage defaults with a single parameter, was used far outside the conditions under which it was reasonable. Ratings models assumed house prices could not fall nationally at the same time. Value-at-Risk models calibrated on calm years understated tail risk. The models were not wrong so much as misused: over-trusted, poorly understood by the people relying on them, and unchallenged.

Then came two instructive failures in 2012. At JPMorgan's Chief Investment Office, the "London Whale" losses of more than $6 billion were compounded by a new VaR model whose implementation involved manual spreadsheet steps and a formula error that roughly halved measured risk. At Knight Capital, a deployment error reactivated obsolete trading code and lost about $440 million in 45 minutes. One is a model-design and validation failure, the other a change-management failure. Both are directly relevant to AI, where retraining and redeployment happen far more often.

The supervisory response defined the discipline banks still use. The Federal Reserve and OCC's SR 11-7 Guidance on Model Risk Management (2011) introduced the idea of effective challenge: critical analysis by objective, informed parties with the competence and authority to force change. It set expectations for development, independent validation, inventory, documentation and governance. The Basel Committee's BCBS 239 (2013) set principles for risk data aggregation and reporting, which govern how accurately and how quickly a bank can tell its board what is happening. Both are the foundation of Chapters 06, 09, 10 and 13 of this guide.

Era IV — The algorithmic accountability awakening (2016–2019)

In 2016 the public conversation changed. In May, ProPublica's "Machine Bias" investigation reported that the COMPAS recidivism score used in US courts had a much higher false-positive rate for Black defendants than for white defendants. The vendor replied that the score was equally well calibrated for both groups. Both claims were true. Within months, Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan, and separately Chouldechova, proved that when base rates differ between groups, no imperfect classifier can satisfy calibration and error-rate balance at the same time. The fairness debate had found its central theorem (Chapter 04).

The same year Microsoft's Tay chatbot was manipulated into producing racist content within a day of launch, and Cathy O'Neil's Weapons of Math Destruction brought algorithmic harm to a general readership. There followed:

  • 2018: GDPR takes effect (May), with rights around automated decision-making and profiling. The Cambridge Analytica revelations expose the manipulation risk of data-driven targeting. Buolamwini and Gebru's Gender Shades finds commercial facial-analysis error rates as high as roughly 35% for darker-skinned women, against under 1% for lighter-skinned men. Reuters reports Amazon's abandoned CV-screening model. Google publishes its AI Principles after employee protests over Project Maven.
  • 2019: The EU High-Level Expert Group publishes its Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI (seven requirements). The OECD adopts the first intergovernmental AI principles in May, later endorsed by the G20. Obermeyer and colleagues show in Science that a widely used US health algorithm under-referred Black patients because it used healthcare cost as a proxy for healthcare need, a textbook problem-specification bias. The Apple Card controversy begins.

Era V — From principles to proof (2020–2026)

By 2019 there were too many principle documents. Jobin, Ienca and Vayena's global review found 84 sets of AI ethics guidelines. They converged on five themes (transparency, justice and fairness, non-maleficence, responsibility and privacy) but diverged sharply on what those meant in practice. The next seven years were about closing that gap, often after high-profile public-sector failures:

  • 2020: England's exam-grading algorithm is withdrawn after it systematically downgraded students from historically lower-performing schools. IBM exits general-purpose facial recognition, and Amazon and Microsoft pause police sales. Timnit Gebru leaves Google after a dispute over a paper on the risks of large language models.
  • 2021: The Dutch government resigns over the childcare-benefits scandal, in which a risk-scoring system wrongly flagged thousands of families, disproportionately those with dual nationality, as fraudsters. The European Commission proposes the AI Act. UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of AI is adopted by all member states.
  • 2022: Microsoft publishes Responsible AI Standard v2 and retires emotion-inference features. ChatGPT launches in November and generative AI becomes a mass-market product within weeks.
  • 2023: NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (January). UK AI white paper (March). Australia's Robodebt Royal Commission reports on an automated debt-recovery scheme found to be unlawful. The PRA publishes SS1/23 on model risk management (May), covering AI and ML explicitly. The Bletchley Declaration (November). ISO/IEC 42001 is published (December). The FTC bans Rite Aid from facial recognition for five years.
  • 2024: Moffatt v. Air Canada establishes that a company owns what its chatbot says. SS1/23 takes effect (May). The EU AI Act enters into force (1 August). The Council of Europe Framework Convention opens for signature (September).
  • 2025: The first International AI Safety Report (January). EU prohibitions and AI-literacy duties apply (February), and general-purpose AI obligations follow (August). Google removes its weapons and surveillance exclusions. Deloitte Australia partially refunds a government report containing fabricated references.
  • 2026: Mobley v. Workday survives a further motion to dismiss (June). The FCA's Mills Review declines AI-specific rules in favour of the Consumer Duty and SM&CR (July). The Digital Omnibus defers EU high-risk obligations to December 2027 and August 2028 (July). A US federal judge rules the Department of Defense's "supply-chain risk" designation of Anthropic unlawful (August), in a dispute over usage restrictions on mass surveillance and autonomous weapons.

What AI ethics can learn from medical ethics

The comparison with medicine in the 1970s is useful. Then, as now, a technology (ventilators, transplantation, IVF) created dilemmas that practitioners were not trained to resolve. The mechanical ventilator produced patients with beating hearts and non-functioning brains, and whether to take their organs was a moral question, not a medical one. Medicine's answer was institutional, and it came in four layers:

Two lessons stand out. First, principles mattered only once they were attached to a body with the power to say no. Research ethics committees can stop a study. Second, the burden of resolving ethical dilemmas was deliberately taken off individual clinicians and given to structures. Engineers, data scientists and product owners should not have to settle fairness trade-offs alone, under deadline pressure. Governance exists to make those decisions explicitly, record them and own them.

Five patterns that repeat across eighty years

  1. Historical data encodes historical judgement. St George's (1988), Amazon (2018) and the Workday allegations (2023–26) are the same failure, forty years apart.
  2. Proxies smuggle in the thing you excluded. Removing race or sex from a model does not remove postcode, name, career gaps or healthcare spending.
  3. Models fail outside the world they were trained in. The Gaussian copula in 2007, Zillow's house-price models in 2021 and pandemic-era data breaks all show it: regime change is a model-risk event.
  4. Scandal comes before structure. Tuskegee led to Belmont, the 2008 crisis to SR 11-7, COMPAS to the fairness literature, and chatbot failures to deployer liability. The cheapest time to build the structure is before your organisation supplies the scandal.
  5. Accountability cannot be outsourced. Neither Air Canada nor the Workday customers could shift responsibility to the machine or the vendor. The law generally follows the decision, not the code.
Chapter 02

Ethical Foundations

Every AI system encodes value judgements: what to optimise, whose errors matter most, which trade-offs are acceptable. Ethics is how an organisation makes those judgements deliberately, consistently and in a way it can defend to customers, regulators and the people who lose out.

In brief

  • There is no value-neutral algorithm. An objective function, a threshold and a training set are each an ethical choice made implicitly. Governance makes the choice explicit.
  • Three classical lenses each catch different failures: consequences (is the net outcome good?), duties and rights (are there lines we must not cross?), and virtue (what would a trustworthy institution do?).
  • Five principles recur in almost every framework: non-maleficence, beneficence, justice, autonomy and explicability. Corporate frameworks usually expand these into eight or nine.
  • Law sets the minimum. Ethics covers what is lawful but still corrosive of trust, which is where most reputational damage happens.
  • The main risk is not bad values but ethics washing: principles with no decision rights, resources or evidence behind them.

Practical ethics: philosophy with a deadline

Practical (or applied) ethics applies moral theory to concrete decisions in medicine, law, business and now AI. It is applied (it must produce a recommendation, not just an analysis), multidisciplinary (philosophy, law, statistics, domain expertise and psychology all contribute), context-sensitive (the same model can be acceptable in marketing and unacceptable in lending), and pluralistic (it combines theories rather than adopting one).

The goal is not to find the single right answer, which often does not exist. It is to reach decisions that are justifiable to everyone affected, including those who lose out. That standard of justifiability is also, in practice, what regulators, courts and journalists apply after the fact.

Why a firm adopts a practical ethics framework

The business case in the source material is candid. Proponents argue that a practical ethics framework:

  • Builds trust and reputation with customers, employees, partners and the public, which in financial services is close to the whole franchise (Chapter 11).
  • Avoids scandals that damage both reputation and the bottom line.
  • Attracts and retains talent, especially engineers and data scientists who increasingly choose employers on values.
  • Strengthens culture: accountability, integrity and willingness to speak up.
  • Supports risk management by giving policies a principled foundation, which reduces safety, privacy and compliance failures.
  • Guides innovation towards products that will survive scrutiny rather than be withdrawn.
  • Promotes long-term thinking, offsetting the quarterly pressures that push towards shipping first and testing later.

Three lenses on right action

Western moral philosophy offers three main families of theory. They are best understood as three questions to ask of any AI decision. The strongest decisions satisfy all three: they produce good consequences, respect rights and duties, and reflect the character of a trustworthy institution.

Consequentialism

“What outcome does this produce?”

An action is right if it produces the best overall consequences. Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill) maximises aggregate welfare. It is forward-looking, quantitative and impartial.

Strength: forces an honest cost–benefit analysis and considers everyone affected.

Weakness: can justify sacrificing individuals for the aggregate, and consequences are uncertain and hard to measure.

Deontology

“What are my duties? What lines must never be crossed?”

Actions are right or wrong in themselves, according to duties and rights. Kant's categorical imperative: act only on maxims you could will as universal law, and treat people always as ends, never merely as means.

Strength: protects individual rights and supplies red lines.

Weakness: rules can conflict and can be rigid when consequences are extreme.

Virtue ethics

“What would a person or institution of good character do?”

From Aristotle: morality is about character traits (practical wisdom, justice, courage, temperance, honesty) built through habit and practice.

Strength: handles novel situations where no rule applies, and it is how culture is actually learned.

Weakness: gives less precise guidance, and virtues can conflict.

Consequentialism in AI: the cost–benefit engine

Most AI business cases are implicitly utilitarian. A fraud model is justified because the losses it prevents outweigh the inconvenience of false alarms. An underwriting model is justified because better risk discrimination lowers average prices. This reasoning is valuable because it makes trade-offs explicit and quantifiable. It is how you choose a decision threshold (Chapter 04).

Its weaknesses are just as important. The metric is subjective: whose welfare counts, and how are unlike harms compared? The horizon is arbitrary: do we count the consequences of the consequences? And aggregate reasoning can hide severe harm to a few. Philosophy's classic example is the surgeon who could kill one patient to save five with their organs. The AI equivalent is a system that is efficient on average but routinely and unappealably wrong about a small, identifiable group.

Rule consequentialism is the standard repair. We judge rules by their consequences and then follow the rules even when breaking them would help in a single case. The surgeon does not harvest organs because a world in which surgeons do so is one in which nobody trusts hospitals. The bank does not deploy a rights-violating model even when it is locally profitable, because a financial system that does so loses the trust it depends on.

Deontology in AI: rights, duties and red lines

Rights, rules and principles are deontological in nature. Much of AI regulation is deontological. The EU AI Act's Article 5 prohibitions (social scoring, manipulative techniques, exploitation of vulnerabilities, untargeted facial scraping and others) are red lines that do not depend on a cost–benefit calculation. So are data-protection rights of access, rectification and objection, and the right not to be subject to certain solely automated decisions.

Kant's instruction to treat people "never merely as a means" is a useful test for AI. A collections model that finds the moment a customer is most psychologically vulnerable and targets repayment prompts at it treats the customer merely as a means to recovery. A model that identifies customers in financial difficulty in order to offer forbearance treats them as ends. The data and technique may be identical. The ethical character is opposite.

Most contemporary deontologists do weigh consequences. They simply hold that consequences are not the only thing that counts, and that some lines should not be crossed even for a net benefit. For an organisation, the practical form of deontology is its acceptable-use policy and prohibited-use list, together with a clear answer to a question the 2025–26 cases made urgent: who is allowed to move a red line, and by what process?

Virtue ethics in AI: culture as a control

Virtue ethics asks "what kind of organisation should we be?" It can seem the least operational of the three, but it may matter most, because rules and calculations run out. No policy anticipates every generative-AI use case an employee will invent next month. What guides them then is culture: whether people habitually ask "should we?" as well as "can we?", whether they feel able to raise concerns, and whether leaders reward the product manager who delayed a launch to fix a fairness problem.

Virtue ethics also explains why process matters. An institution shows practical wisdom by building forums where hard trade-offs are debated by people with different expertise and recorded, and not by one engineer at 11pm before a release. The source modules make the same point: an ethics committee that weighs consequentialist, deontological and virtue considerations supports both procedural and outcome fairness.

The five core principles of AI ethics

Floridi and Cowls (2019) observed that most AI ethics codes reduce to the four principles of bioethics plus one new one. The source modules adopt this five-principle structure.

1. Non-maleficence: do no (unjustified) harm

This is the obligation not to harm others and, just as important, not to impose unjustified risks of harm. Subjecting customers to an untested model is wrong even if it happens to cause no harm, because it imposes a risk they did not agree to. Non-maleficence does not forbid all risk. Surgery and clinical trials carry risk that their benefits justify. What matters is who decides and who bears the risk. It is more acceptable to impose risk on people who stand to benefit from the action, and least acceptable when those who bear the risk gain nothing, as with a fraud model whose false positives fall on customers while the fraud savings accrue to the bank.

2. Beneficence: do good

Beneficence is a positive duty to benefit others, going beyond avoiding harm. It is often mistaken for a purely consequentialist idea, but deontological frameworks include it too. It is bounded by resources, competing obligations and the recipient's autonomy: an unwanted "benefit" imposed on someone disrespects them. For AI, the minimum form of beneficence is that a system should solve a real problem for the people it affects, not only for the firm. The UK Consumer Duty's requirement to "enable and support retail customers to pursue their financial objectives" is, in effect, a regulatory form of beneficence.

3. Justice: fairness in distribution, procedure and redress

Justice has several dimensions, each of which maps onto an AI control:

Theories of justice disagree about what a fair distribution is. Egalitarian, utilitarian, meritocratic and needs-based principles give different answers. That disagreement is reproduced exactly in the mathematics of fairness, where different metrics encode different theories of justice and cannot all be satisfied (Chapter 04). There is broad agreement on one point: people should not be disadvantaged on the basis of morally irrelevant characteristics.

4. Autonomy: respect people's capacity to choose

Autonomy is the capacity to hold beliefs and make decisions that are genuinely one's own (the authenticity dimension) and the freedom to act on them (the agency dimension). Delegating a task to AI does not in itself reduce autonomy, since we delegate to people constantly. It does so when delegation is uncontrolled, when meaningful human oversight is lost, or when the system shapes our choices without our awareness.

The sharpest threat is manipulation: influence that bypasses a person's rational agency by exploiting cognitive biases, emotional states or vulnerabilities. Facebook's 2014 emotional-contagion experiment, which altered users' feeds without consent to test whether mood spread, and Cambridge Analytica's psychographic targeting are the standard cautionary tales. In retail finance the risks are specific: dark patterns in onboarding, hyper-personalised pricing that targets inertia, "nudges" optimised for the firm's revenue rather than the customer's goals, and conversational agents that build false rapport. The EU AI Act prohibits AI that deploys subliminal, manipulative or deceptive techniques causing significant harm, or that exploits vulnerabilities due to age, disability or social or economic situation.

5. Explicability: intelligibility plus accountability

Explicability joins two ideas: intelligibility (how does it work?) and accountability (who is responsible for how it works?). It serves the other four principles: you cannot judge whether a system is harmful, beneficial, just or respectful of autonomy without some understanding of it. It also underpins trust, since people reasonably distrust decisions they cannot question. Chapter 05 covers the techniques. The ethical point is that what counts as a good explanation depends on who it is owed to: a customer needs actionable reasons, a regulator needs evidence of lawful and consistent treatment, and a validator needs mechanistic understanding.

Privacy as an ethical issue

Privacy deserves separate treatment because data is the raw material of AI. In the source modules' framing, we have privacy with respect to someone to the extent that they lack access to our personal data. Privacy matters because knowledge is power: the more an institution knows about you, the more easily it can influence, exclude or exploit you.

It helps to separate wrongs from harms. A privacy violation can be wrong even if no tangible harm follows, because it disregards a person's rights and treats them as a means. Harms, when they come, can be severe: discrimination, blackmail, identity theft, public exposure. For the institution, every unnecessary personal data point is a potential lawsuit, fine or breach.

That gives the most useful metaphor in the privacy literature: personal data is a toxic asset. It is cheap to collect, valuable, sensitive and very hard to keep safe, and attackers have the advantage because they choose the time and method while defenders must guard against everything. An organisation that collects more than it needs creates its own risk. Six operating principles follow:

  1. Data minimisation: collect only what the stated purpose requires, and only when the benefit to the data subject outweighs the risk imposed on them.
  2. Contextual integrity (Nissenbaum): data given in one context carries that context's norms. Transaction data given to a bank to make a payment should not silently become marketing, pricing or training data for an unrelated purpose.
  3. Control: make it easy to withdraw consent, access data and the inferences made about you, port and delete.
  4. Deletion and retention limits: personal data should have an expiry date. Old data is both riskier and less accurate.
  5. Security by design: encryption, access control, pseudonymisation, and privacy-enhancing technologies such as differential privacy, federated learning and synthetic data where appropriate.
  6. The right to be forgotten: an erasure right that is hard to honour once personal data has been absorbed into model weights. Generative AI raises this problem sharply.

Ethics and law: the floor and the ceiling

Law and ethics overlap but are not the same. Law sets minimum standards of behaviour so that institutions can function. It is narrow, slow to change and often silent on new technology. Ethics is more ambitious: it asks what kind of institution and society we want. Abandoning a friend in need is lawful but wrong. In AI, much of the conduct that destroys trust is lawful, or legally untested, when it happens.

The Apple Card case shows this. New York's regulator found no unlawful discrimination in the credit-limit algorithm, but criticised the lack of transparency and poor customer service when people asked why. The firm complied with the law and still suffered serious reputational harm. For a regulated firm there is also a structural point: in the UK the Consumer Duty's requirement to "act in good faith" and "avoid foreseeable harm" moves a good deal of what used to be ethics into enforceable conduct regulation.

Five tensions no framework resolves for you

Accuracy vs fairness

Different mathematical definitions of fairness cannot all be met at once when base rates differ, and constraints usually cost some accuracy. Choose one, justify it and record it.

Performance vs explainability

The most accurate models are often the least interpretable. Sometimes a slightly weaker glass-box model is the right answer, as it usually is for credit decisions.

Speed vs assurance

Competitive pressure pushes towards shipping before testing is complete. Tiered, proportionate governance is the answer, not the absence of governance.

Reputation vs substance

Ethics washing: principles and boards used as reputational cover without decision rights, budget or evidence.

Company values vs customer or state demands

Who decides acceptable use once a model is sold, especially to governments? Google's 2025 revision and the 2026 Anthropic–DoD dispute put this question at the centre.

Personalisation vs autonomy

Relevance and manipulation use the same machinery. The line is whether the system serves the customer's goals or exploits their biases.

Questions a board should ask about its AI ethics framework

  • Can you name a product we changed, delayed or did not launch because of our AI principles in the last year? If not, how do we know they bite?
  • Who can approve an exception to our prohibited-use list, and has anyone done so?
  • Does our AI ethics or risk committee have the authority to stop a launch, or only to advise?
  • How do we find out when a customer is harmed by an AI decision, and how quickly?
  • What would we be uncomfortable seeing on the front page, even though it is lawful?
Chapter 03

The AI Risk Landscape

Toasters burn, planes crash and diagnostic tools fail, and AI is no exception. What makes AI risk distinctive is its range (the same technique appears in thousands of uses), its stakes (more and more consequential decisions), its pace (it outruns the regulation meant to govern it) and its interconnection (one failure mode sets off others).

In brief

  • Bias can enter at every stage of the lifecycle: problem specification, data, modelling and deployment. Removing a protected attribute does not remove its proxies.
  • Consistency is double-edged: an algorithm applies its bias systematically, at scale.
  • Generative and agentic AI add new risk classes: hallucination, prompt injection, data leakage, excessive agency and supplier concentration on a few foundation-model providers.
  • Reputational damage from AI has three main causes (privacy breaches, algorithmic bias and lack of explainability) and draws two kinds of criticism: incompetence or misaligned values.
  • Most of these risks are not new categories for a bank. They are model, conduct, operational, third-party and data risks that arrive faster and less visibly.

1. Algorithmic bias

The word "bias" means different things in different disciplines. To a statistician it is a systematic error between an estimate and the truth. To a psychologist it is a systematic departure from rational judgement: automation bias, for example, is trusting a machine more than the evidence justifies. In public debate it usually means unfair treatment of people. The source modules adopt a deliberately neutral definition:

Algorithmic bias is a systematic deviation of an algorithm's output, performance or impact relative to some norm, aim, standard or baseline.

This neutral definition is useful because not all bias is problematic. A credit model is supposed to treat applicants with strong repayment histories differently from those with defaults. That bias is its purpose. The ethical and legal problem arises when a bias disadvantages people for unjustifiable reasons: characteristics that are morally irrelevant to the decision, or protected by law. The reverse also holds: a statistically "unbiased" algorithm can still be harmful, for example if it charges everyone an exorbitant fee.

Bias can be explicit (a developer deliberately encodes a prejudice, which is rare) or implicit (it enters unnoticed through design choices or data, which is common). Implicit bias is the governance challenge, because nobody intends it and nobody notices it without testing.

1a. Problem specification and feature selection

Before any data is touched, the framing of the problem can build in unfairness. Target-variable choice is the most consequential decision in most models. The Obermeyer (2019) health algorithm predicted future healthcare cost as a proxy for health need. Because less money had historically been spent on Black patients with the same level of illness, the algorithm systematically under-prioritised them. The model was accurate at predicting cost. It was answering the wrong question.

A banking example from the source material: a lender that aims to eliminate all credit risk and lends only where it is certain of repayment ends up serving only the affluent. The objective itself produces exclusion. Adjusting the target to accept a reasonable level of risk is an ethical as well as commercial choice.

Criteria also have to be operationalised through proxies. "Productive employee" becomes "total annual sales", which quietly penalises part-time workers, who in many labour markets are disproportionately women. "Team player" may be dropped because it is hard to measure, and if that is an area where some groups excel, dropping it disadvantages them. And endogeneity means the job specification itself shapes who applies, so the training population is already selected.

Finally, less can be more. A widely used recidivism tool took 137 inputs, and researchers showed that nearly equivalent predictions could be made with just two (age and number of prior convictions). Every extra feature is an extra chance for a proxy to slip in and an extra obstacle to explanation.

1b. Data: historical, representation, measurement and selective labels

  • Historical bias. Even perfectly representative data reflects the world as it was, including its discrimination. A lending dataset spanning decades will show successful loans skewed to men, because women were for a long time excluded from full participation in credit markets. A model trained on it learns that pattern and, through feedback loops, amplifies it: fewer loans to women mean fewer positive repayment records for women in the next training set.
  • Representation (sampling) bias. The sample does not match the target population. Facial-recognition and medical datasets dominated by white or European subjects produce models that perform worse on everyone else, and worst of all at intersections (Gender Shades found error rates for darker-skinned women dozens of times higher than for lighter-skinned men).
  • Measurement bias. The measurement process differs across groups. If doctors spend more time examining wealthy patients, early-stage disease is recorded more often for them, and a model concludes that the rich are sicker earlier.
  • Selective labels. Historical data rarely shows counterfactual outcomes. A bank knows how its approved borrowers performed but not how its rejected applicants would have done. The model keeps selecting people like those previously approved and never learns it was wrong about the rest. (Reject inference techniques in credit scoring exist precisely to address this.)
  • Temporal and regime bias. Data from 2010–2019 describes a world of office commuting that did not survive 2020. The source modules also note the pandemic break in official unemployment statistics, where the flaw was disclosed only in footnotes.

1c. Data composition: the balancing dilemma

Statistical learning optimises for the typical case. If 3% of patients in a medical dataset are pregnant and need different treatment, the model will underperform for them simply because it has seen too few. Remedies include re-sampling (over-sampling the minority, under-sampling the majority, or bootstrap combinations), synthetic data generation, cost-sensitive learning (penalising minority-class errors more) and ensemble designs that use every minority example in each sub-model.

There is a subtle point here. A balanced dataset is not a representative one. Balancing a hiring dataset to 50/50 by sex when the applicant pool was 80/20 improves the model's ability to learn about the minority group but makes the dataset less like reality, and it can distort calibration if not corrected. Deciding which groups to balance on (protected characteristics, or clinically relevant ones such as pregnancy or diabetes) is itself a legal, ethical and practical judgement. There is no universal answer.

1d. Modelling, validation and design

  • Pre-processing artefacts. Word embeddings learned from web text encode stereotypes: the notorious finding is that an embedding completed "man is to computer programmer as woman is to…" with "homemaker". Any NLP model built on such embeddings inherits them.
  • The objective function is a value judgement. Minimising total error treats a missed cancer and a false alarm as equally bad. For a skin-lesion screener, a missed melanoma is far worse, so we accept more false alarms. For a brain-surgery recommender, a false positive means invasive surgery, so the balance is different. The loss function is where ethics becomes arithmetic.
  • Benchmark bias. Testing against public benchmark datasets that do not represent your deployment population gives false comfort.
  • Popularity bias. A product-recommendation engine that favours popular items creates a loop in which exposure drives popularity regardless of quality or suitability. In financial products this is a Consumer Duty issue.

1e. Deployment: context shift and automation bias

An unbiased model can still cause biased outcomes once deployed. Context transfer is one route: a recidivism predictor repurposed for sentencing length, or a vendor's hiring model whose idea of a "good employee" differs from yours. Automation bias is the other, and it is one of the most important and least controlled risks in enterprise AI. Studies consistently find that people defer to algorithmic suggestions even when told the tool is experimental. The reasons are convenience, the appearance of objectivity, self-doubt, and the fact that deferring to the algorithm shields the human from blame. Going against the machine and being wrong is judged more harshly than following it and being wrong. The result is a "human in the loop" who is a rubber stamp.

Assurance angle

Testing whether human oversight is real

Ask for override rates by reviewer, team and period. A rate near 0% suggests rubber-stamping; a rate near 50% suggests the model is not trusted or not fit for purpose. Then sample overrides and non-overrides and ask: did the reviewer have information the model lacked? How long did review take? Were reviewers trained on the model's known limitations? Chapter 12 treats override rate as a two-sided key risk indicator.

2. Opacity and the black-box problem

Some models (decision trees, scorecards, sparse linear models) are interpretable: a human can follow how inputs become outputs. Others (deep neural networks, large ensembles, LLMs with hundreds of billions of parameters) are opaque even to their builders. Opacity has a technical side (how do we extract an explanation?) and a philosophical side (what counts as a good explanation?). Opacity also comes in three forms that have nothing to do with the maths:

  • Secrecy: people do not know an algorithm is being used on them, or even that it exists (consumer "scores" few people have heard of).
  • Confidentiality: people know an algorithm is used but cannot see how it works or why it reached its result. There are sometimes valid reasons: publishing a fraud or spam model helps attackers evade it, and vendors protect trade secrets. But confidentiality becomes a serious problem when it stops a deployer from assessing a vendor's model's impact on its own customers.
  • Specialised knowledge: even with code and data available, understanding them takes expertise most affected people, and many managers, do not have.

Chapter 05 covers the techniques for managing opacity: SHAP, LIME, counterfactuals and interpretable-by-design models.

3. Autonomy and manipulation

Recommendation and ranking systems shape what we see, and the data behind them lets them predict and influence what we do. That capability serves relevance and convenience, and it also enables manipulation. The risk is hard to govern because manipulation is hard to operationalise: there is no agreed metric for it. Regulators have responded with transparency duties and outright prohibitions on subliminal and exploitative techniques. For a bank the practical controls are design review of customer journeys (dark patterns), objective-function review (is the model optimising for customer outcome or firm revenue?), and outcome testing against Consumer Duty expectations.

4. Safety and wellbeing

In March 2018 an Uber self-driving test vehicle struck and killed a pedestrian in Tempe, Arizona. The investigation found a layered failure: the perception system repeatedly misclassified the pedestrian, an emergency braking capability had been disabled, and the safety driver was distracted. This is automation bias in its most lethal form. The lessons carry over to every high-stakes AI deployment:

  • Test in realistic conditions, including rare and adversarial ones, before relying on a system.
  • Design for vigilance. Require periodic human input, use attention checks, and make disagreement cheap.
  • Plan for skill atrophy. When people move from active decision-making to passive monitoring, their expertise decays and they become less able to catch the errors they are there to catch.
  • Consider vulnerable users. AI mental-health tools can produce responses rated as more empathetic than average human replies, but the benefit fades once users learn the reply was machine-generated, and these systems have no actual empathy. Lawsuits filed since 2024 allege that consumer chatbots contributed to harm to teenagers. Foreseeable misuse by vulnerable people belongs in the risk assessment.

The source modules state a golden rule: just because we can does not mean we should. They add a second: involve diverse stakeholders (domain experts, the public, ethicists and policymakers) in high-stakes AI design.

Agentic AI: the 2025–26 frontier

AI agents plan, call tools, move data and take actions with limited human involvement. The FCA's July 2026 Mills Review names "agent-led consumer journeys" as one of four shifts that will reshape retail finance by 2030: agents that compare, switch and buy financial products for customers. Agents change the risk calculus in three ways. Errors become actions, not just outputs, and some are irreversible. Attack surfaces multiply, since every document, website or API an agent reads is a potential injection vector. And accountability blurs: when a customer's agent negotiates with a bank's agent, who is responsible for the outcome? The controls are familiar from payments and privileged access (limits, segregation of duties, approval for high-value or irreversible actions, full audit trails), now applied to software actors.

6. Reputational risk

Research by Holweg, Younger and Wen (2022) on AI failures found that reputational damage clusters around three causes: privacy breaches (Cambridge Analytica), algorithmic bias (the Dutch childcare-benefits scandal, which brought down a government) and lack of explainability (customers refused a loan with no way to learn why). They also distinguish two kinds of criticism, and the distinction matters for crisis response:

Criticism of competence

“They didn't know how to do this safely.” Technical failures, unfair outputs, weak explanations, breaches.

Response: transparency about what went wrong and why; visible technical remediation; independent verification.

Criticism of value alignment

“They knew, and did it anyway.” Facebook's emotional-contagion experiment, where the firm knowingly ran a study without consent, or obvious lack of due diligence.

Response: much harder. The cause is governance or culture, so the fix must be organisational and visible: leadership change, new decision rights, external oversight.

The two often blur. A data breach can reflect incompetence, or a decision not to invest in security because the firm did not value customers' privacy. Stakeholders tend to assume the worse interpretation unless the firm can show otherwise, which is one reason evidence of governance, such as records of decisions, testing and escalation, has reputational as well as regulatory value.

Mitigation strategies from the source modules: continuous monitoring and evaluation; privacy and security by design; fairness auditing; proactive transparency; demonstrating the will to change after an incident; ethical frameworks developed with experts (many generic frameworks are too vague to help in ambiguous cases); ongoing stakeholder engagement; and an AI-specific crisis management plan prepared in advance.

7. Model risk

Every AI system is a model and inherits classical model risk: fundamental error (wrong design, bad data, implementation bugs) and misuse (applying a sound model outside its domain, or misreading its outputs). Zillow Offers (2021) is the standard AI-era example. House-price forecasting models could not keep pace with a turning market, and the business was wound down with large write-downs and job losses. Model risk is business risk, and it can be existential for a business line. Chapter 06 covers measurement and Chapter 09 the controls.

8. Third-party and concentration risk

Most enterprise AI is bought, not built: embedded in HR platforms, CRM suites, fraud engines, productivity tools, and increasingly running on a handful of foundation models. Workday's customers, Air Canada and Rite Aid all show that you own your vendors' AI in the eyes of courts and regulators. Concentration adds a systemic dimension: if much of the financial sector relies on a few model providers and cloud platforms, a single outage, compromise or silent model change becomes a sector-wide event. That is why the EU's DORA and the UK's critical third parties regime matter for AI (Chapter 07).

9. Frontier, existential and global risks

At the far end of the spectrum is concern that highly capable AI could cause catastrophic or existential harm. That could come through misuse (uplift for biological or cyber attacks), misalignment (a system pursuing goals that diverge from human values: Bostrom's thought experiment of an AI that turns everything into paperclips), or competitive "race" dynamics that erode safety margins. Sceptics argue that current systems remain narrow, that governance will adapt, and that every major technology has attracted inflated fears; early railway critics worried that passengers would suffocate at speed. Frontier developers now publish safety frameworks (Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework) that tie capability thresholds to escalating safeguards, and the EU AI Act imposes obligations on general-purpose models with systemic risk.

Wider societal risks are more immediate for most enterprises:

  • Labour-market disruption. Estimates vary widely. Frey and Osborne's much-cited study put 47% of US employment at high risk of computerisation; later studies found far less. Post-2022 estimates focus on exposure of tasks, including high-skill ones, rather than whole jobs. For a large employer this is a workforce planning, reskilling and social-licence question.
  • Inequality between people who can work with AI and those who cannot, and between nations at the centre of AI development and those at the periphery.
  • Misinformation and information integrity: synthetic media, deepfake fraud (a major concern for bank authentication and payment controls) and the erosion of trust in evidence itself.
  • Privacy and surveillance as the data economy's appetite for training data grows.
  • Environmental impact of training and inference compute, now an explicit consideration in several frameworks and in sustainability reporting.

The interconnection principle

AI risks are rarely independent. Lack of transparency hides bias. Bias produces discrimination claims. Discrimination claims become regulatory findings and headlines. A hallucination in a customer letter is at once a model failure, a conduct breach and a complaint. Controls should therefore be designed around failure pathways, not risk silos, and assurance should test the pathway end to end.

Chapter 04

The Mathematics of Fairness

“Is the model fair?” sounds like a yes/no question. It is not. There are at least a dozen formal definitions of fairness, each encoding a different theory of justice, and a mathematical theorem shows that the most important of them cannot all hold at once. This chapter builds the ideas from a confusion matrix up, so the trade-off can be seen, measured and then decided.

In brief

  • Individual fairness treats like cases alike. Group fairness compares statistics across groups. Both are needed, and they can conflict.
  • The three families of group fairness are independence (equal approval rates), separation (equal error rates) and sufficiency (a score means the same thing for everyone).
  • When groups have different base rates, which is almost always, an imperfect model cannot satisfy all three. This is a theorem, not an engineering shortfall.
  • So fairness cannot be automated. Choosing a metric is a governance decision that should be made by accountable people, documented and revisited, not left to a data scientist's default setting.
  • Excluding protected characteristics from a model does not make it fair. Proxies carry the signal. You usually need the protected data to test for fairness.
PractitionerAuditBoard: read the box on choosing a metric

Notation

We use one running example throughout: a lender's model that decides whether to approve a loan application. Let

  • \(Y \in \{0,1\}\) be the true outcome: \(Y=1\) if the applicant would repay (a "qualified" applicant).
  • \(S \in [0,1]\) be the model's score: its estimate of the probability of repayment.
  • \(\hat{Y} = \mathbb{1}[S \ge t]\) be the decision: approve if the score clears threshold \(t\).
  • \(A\) be a protected or sensitive attribute (for example sex, age band or ethnicity), with groups \(a\) and \(b\).
  • \(p_a = P(Y=1 \mid A=a)\) be the base rate (prevalence of the positive outcome) in group \(a\).

Individual fairness: treating like cases alike

The oldest conception of fairness goes back to Aristotle: treat like cases alike. Dwork et al. (2012) formalised it as a Lipschitz condition. If \(d_X\) measures how similar two individuals are in task-relevant ways, and \(d_Y\) measures how different their treatment is, a fair classifier \(f\) satisfies

$$ d_Y\big(f(x), f(x')\big) \;\le\; L \cdot d_X(x, x') \quad \text{for all individuals } x, x'. $$

Similar people receive similar outcomes; the constant \(L\) bounds how sharply outcomes can change as people become less alike.

The whole difficulty is hidden in \(d_X\): who counts as similar? The source modules use college admissions. If merit means grades, two applicants with equal grades are alike. But suppose one achieved those grades while overcoming serious socio-economic or physical hurdles. Is she "alike" the other applicant, or more meritorious? Reasonable people disagree, and an algorithm forces the designer to encode one answer explicitly. Individual fairness is essential but not self-executing: someone has to define the similarity metric, and that is a normative choice.

The accuracy paradox

Why accuracy is not enough

A recruiter screens 100 applicants, of whom 10 are suitable. A model that rejects everyone gets 90 true negatives and 10 false negatives: 90% accuracy and zero usefulness. With imbalanced classes (fraud, default, rare diseases), accuracy mainly reflects the majority class. Always report the class-conditional rates (TPR, FPR, PPV) and, for scores, a threshold-free measure such as AUC (Chapter 06).

Demographic parity

Demographic parity requires equal selection rates, with no reference to the true outcome \(Y\). That is its strength and its weakness. It is useful when true outcomes are unobservable or themselves biased: credit outcomes arrive years after the decision, fraud labels are sparse and shifting, and historical "hired / not hired" labels encode past discrimination. But it is blind to accuracy. It can be satisfied by a model that is wrong about everyone in one group, as long as it approves the right proportion. When base rates genuinely differ for reasons relevant to the decision, forcing equal selection rates means either approving people likely to be harmed by unaffordable credit or refusing qualified people in the other group.

In practice parity is usually measured as a ratio, the adverse (or disparate) impact ratio:

$$ \text{AIR} = \frac{P(\hat Y = 1 \mid A = \text{protected})}{P(\hat Y = 1 \mid A = \text{reference})}. $$

The US four-fifths rule (EEOC Uniform Guidelines, 1978) treats an AIR below 0.8 as preliminary evidence of adverse impact in employment. New York City's Local Law 144 requires published "impact ratios" from bias audits of automated employment decision tools. Many banks use 0.8 as a screening threshold that triggers investigation, not as proof of fairness or unfairness.

Equal opportunity and equalised odds

Hardt, Price and Srebro (2016) proposed equal opportunity: equal true-positive rates. Among applicants who would in fact repay, each group has the same chance of being approved. This fits contexts where a missed positive is the main harm (a missed diagnosis, a qualified applicant refused). Equalised odds adds equal false-positive rates, so the burden of wrongful approval (for example unaffordable lending) is also shared equally. Both require knowing \(Y\), and both become hard to achieve without cost when base rates differ.

Predictive parity and calibration

Predictive parity requires equal PPV: of those approved, the same proportion repay in each group. Calibration within groups is the score-level version: a score of 0.7 means a 70% chance of repayment whether you are in group \(a\) or \(b\). Lenders and model validators usually treat calibration as non-negotiable, because a miscalibrated score systematically over- or under-states risk for a group, which is a form of inaccuracy that is also unfair. This is the criterion the vendor of COMPAS cited in its defence.

Is this model fair? It depends entirely on the question:

  • Accuracy is identical (84%) for both groups. Many would stop here. That would be a mistake.
  • Demographic parity fails badly: AIR = 46.0 / 66.0 = 0.70, well under the four-fifths screen.
  • Equal opportunity shows a modest gap: qualified B applicants are approved 80.0% of the time against 85.7% for A, a TPR difference of 5.7 points.
  • Equalised odds also fails on FPR (20% vs 12%). Unqualified A applicants are more likely to be wrongly approved, which may itself be a harm if it means unaffordable debt.
  • Predictive parity is close: 90.9% vs 87.0% of approvals repay.

Most of the gap in selection rates comes from the difference in base rates (70% vs 50%), which the model is partly tracking. Whether that is acceptable depends on why the base rates differ. If they reflect real, relevant differences in repayment capacity, sufficiency-type fairness is arguably right. If they reflect past discrimination (thin credit files, lower historical incomes because of pay discrimination, redlined postcodes), the model is reproducing injustice and parity-type constraints have a stronger claim. The data cannot settle this. It is a judgement.

The impossibility theorem

Why not just demand that all the criteria hold? Because, with unequal base rates, they cannot. Two 2016–17 results, from Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan and from Chouldechova, made this precise, and it is short enough to derive here.

Start from the definitions. Among \(N_a\) people in group \(a\), \(p_a N_a\) are positive. Then \(TP = \text{TPR}\cdot p_a N_a\). From the definition of PPV, \(FP = TP \cdot \frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\). Dividing by the number of negatives, \((1-p_a)N_a\), gives an identity that holds for any classifier:

$$ \boxed{\;\text{FPR}_a \;=\; \frac{p_a}{1-p_a}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}_a}{\text{PPV}_a}\cdot \text{TPR}_a\;} $$

Chouldechova's identity: the false-positive rate is fixed once the base rate, precision and recall are fixed.

Now suppose we insist on predictive parity (\(\text{PPV}_a = \text{PPV}_b\)) and equal opportunity (\(\text{TPR}_a = \text{TPR}_b\)). The second and third factors are then identical across groups, so

$$ \frac{\text{FPR}_a}{\text{FPR}_b} \;=\; \frac{p_a/(1-p_a)}{p_b/(1-p_b)}. $$

If \(p_a \ne p_b\), the false-positive rates must differ, so equalised odds fails. The only escapes are equal base rates, or a perfect classifier (FPR = 0 and PPV = 1 for everyone). Neither occurs in practice. A similar argument shows that calibration and demographic parity are incompatible when base rates differ: if scores mean the same thing in both groups and the groups genuinely differ in prevalence, they must receive different score distributions.

Go deeper: Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan, stated formally

Consider a risk score \(S\) and three conditions:

  1. Calibration within groups: for each group and each score bin \(s\), the fraction of positives among people assigned \(s\) equals \(s\).
  2. Balance for the positive class: the average score given to positives is equal across groups, \(E[S\mid Y{=}1, A{=}a] = E[S \mid Y{=}1, A{=}b]\).
  3. Balance for the negative class: \(E[S\mid Y{=}0, A{=}a] = E[S \mid Y{=}0, A{=}b]\).

Theorem: all three can hold simultaneously only if (i) prediction is perfect, or (ii) base rates are equal. Even approximate versions force the violations to grow with the base-rate gap. Conditions 2 and 3 are the score-level analogues of equal TPR and FPR, so the theorem is the continuous-score version of the identity above.

The COMPAS debate is this theorem in action. The tool was roughly calibrated across races (Northpointe's defence), and therefore, given different recorded re-offence base rates, it had to have unequal false-positive rates (ProPublica's finding). Both parties were right about the numbers and disagreed about which criterion mattered.

Error-rate trade-offs within a single group

Even without fairness constraints, choosing a threshold trades one error for another. A fraud model could flag every transaction and achieve perfect sensitivity (no missed fraud) with an intolerable false-positive rate, or flag nothing and achieve a zero false-positive rate while missing all fraud. The ROC curve (Chapter 06) traces every achievable pair of (FPR, TPR). Each group has its own ROC curve, and equalised odds can only be reached at points both curves can reach. Hardt et al. showed that where the curves differ, this may require group-specific thresholds or randomising between thresholds for one group, which raises legal questions of its own (see below).

For the board: what “we tested for fairness” should mean

A credible answer names (1) the primary criterion chosen and why; (2) the groups tested, including intersections where data allows; (3) the tolerance and what happens when it is breached; (4) the trade-off accepted, such as “we accept a 0.4-point AUC reduction to bring the TPR gap under 3 points”; and (5) who approved that trade-off. “The model does not use protected characteristics” is not a fairness test.

Fairness through unawareness, and why it fails

The instinctive fix is to remove protected attributes from the model. This is called fairness through unawareness, and on its own it does not work, for two reasons. First, proxies: postcode correlates with ethnicity, first name with sex and ethnicity, career gaps with sex and disability, device type and shopping patterns with age and income. Amazon's recruiting model learned to penalise the word "women's" and the names of all-women's colleges after sex itself was excluded. Second, without the protected attribute you cannot measure any group-fairness metric, so you cannot know whether you have a problem.

A simple and powerful test is the proxy model: train a model to predict the protected attribute \(A\) from the features the credit model uses. If it reaches an AUC well above 0.5, the features jointly encode \(A\), and the credit model can discriminate on \(A\) without ever seeing it. Feature-level measures such as mutual information \(I(X_j; A)\) or correlation with \(A\) help locate the worst proxies.

The legal knot: you need the data you are not allowed to use

In many jurisdictions, using a protected characteristic in a decision is direct discrimination, and group-specific thresholds are generally unlawful disparate treatment in US lending and employment, with UK Equality Act positive-action exceptions being narrow. Yet you need that characteristic to test for indirect discrimination. Collecting it raises special-category data questions under the GDPR (Article 9) and UK GDPR, although the UK DPA 2018 provides conditions for equality-of-opportunity monitoring. Common practical solutions: segregated testing datasets with strict access controls; voluntary self-identification for monitoring only; and statistical inference of group membership for testing (for example BISG, Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding, used by US regulators), with its own error rates acknowledged. The EU AI Act (Article 10(5)) explicitly permits processing special-category data to detect and correct bias in high-risk systems, subject to safeguards.

Mitigation: pre-, in- and post-processing

Before training

Pre-processing

Fix the data. Re-weighing (Kamiran & Calders) weights each (group, outcome) cell so that \(A\) and \(Y\) are independent in the training data. Resampling, synthetic augmentation, label correction where historical labels are known to be biased, and fair representations that remove information about \(A\).

Pro: model-agnostic. Con: indirect; can hurt calibration.

During training

In-processing

Add fairness to the objective: minimise \( \mathcal{L}(\theta) + \lambda \cdot \text{Unfairness}(\theta)\), or optimise subject to constraints (for example Agarwal et al.'s reductions approach). Adversarial debiasing trains the model so that an adversary cannot recover \(A\) from its outputs.

Pro: principled trade-off controlled by \(\lambda\). Con: needs \(A\) at training time; harder to explain.

After training

Post-processing

Adjust decisions: group-specific thresholds or randomised mixing (Hardt et al.) to equalise odds, or reject-option classification near the boundary.

Pro: simple, works on black-box vendor models. Con: uses \(A\) at decision time, which is legally sensitive and often impermissible in lending and hiring.

The most effective mitigation in regulated finance is often none of these. It is feature and target review: remove or constrain features that act as proxies without adding legitimate predictive value, reconsider the target (is "repaid" what we want, or "repaid without hardship"?), and prefer interpretable model classes where decisions must be explained. Then document the less discriminatory alternative search. US fair-lending practice asks whether an alternative model with similar predictive power but less disparate impact was available, a standard increasingly echoed in UK and EU supervisory expectations.

Statistics before accusations: sampling error in fairness metrics

Fairness metrics are estimates, often from small subgroups. An observed TPR gap of 6 points could be noise. For two proportions \(\hat p_1, \hat p_2\) from samples of size \(n_1, n_2\), an approximate 95% confidence interval for the difference is

$$ (\hat p_1 - \hat p_2) \;\pm\; 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat p_2(1-\hat p_2)}{n_2}}. $$

For the worked example's TPR gap (85.7% of 700 vs 80.0% of 500), the interval is roughly 5.7 ± 4.4 points: statistically significant, but only just. For intersectional subgroups of a few dozen people, intervals can be ±20 points or more. Good practice reports both statistical and practical significance, pools periods to accumulate evidence, and sets monitoring rules that require persistence (for example a breach in two consecutive quarters) before escalation. Chapter 12 applies control-chart logic to this.

Why fairness cannot be automated

The source modules conclude plainly that fairness is ultimately a moral judgement, not a mathematical one. The mathematics is still indispensable: it shows exactly what is being traded and makes the choice explicit and auditable. But no toolkit (AIF360, Fairlearn, Aequitas) can decide for you whether equal approval rates or equal error rates is the right standard for your product. If fairness cannot be automated, fairness auditing cannot be fully automated either, and AI cannot on its own solve the fairness problems it creates. It needs a process: a forum where consequentialist, deontological and virtue-based considerations are weighed, a decision recorded, and outcomes monitored. That process is the subject of Chapters 08–10.

Auditing a fairness assessment: the evidence checklist

  • Documented rationale for the primary fairness criterion and tolerance, approved at the right level.
  • Group definitions, data source for protected attributes, and treatment of missing or inferred attributes.
  • Results with confidence intervals, including intersectional cuts where sample sizes permit.
  • Proxy analysis of the feature set, and a less-discriminatory-alternative search.

Chapter 05

Explainability & Transparency

A decision that cannot be explained cannot be challenged, corrected or trusted. In lending, explanation has been a legal duty for fifty years. The difficulty today is that the most accurate models are often the hardest to explain, and the popular explanation tools have limits that matter a great deal for assurance.

In brief

  • Interpretability is built in: a human can follow the model's logic directly. Explainability is added afterwards: a technique approximates why a model did what it did.
  • Different audiences need different explanations. Customers need actionable reasons, regulators need evidence of lawful, consistent treatment, and validators need mechanistic understanding.
  • Shapley values (SHAP) are the most rigorous general-purpose attribution method, but they are not causal, they are sensitive to correlated features, and they can be manipulated.
  • Counterfactual explanations (“you would have been approved if…”) are closest to what customers want, provided they point to things the customer can actually change.
  • For high-stakes decisions, an interpretable model that is almost as accurate is often better than an opaque model with a post-hoc explanation.
PractitionerAuditBoard

Four distinctions

  • Interpretable vs explainable. An inherently interpretable model (scorecard, sparse linear model, shallow tree, generalised additive model) shows how inputs affect outputs by construction. Ex-post explainability applies a separate technique to an opaque model after training.
  • Local vs global. A local explanation covers one decision: why was this applicant declined? A global explanation covers the model overall: which factors matter most across the portfolio, and how?
  • Model-specific vs model-agnostic. Tree-path explanations and neural-network gradients use the model's internals. LIME, KernelSHAP and counterfactual search treat the model as a black box, so they also work on vendor models.
  • Explanation vs justification. An explanation says why the model decided. A justification says why that decision is acceptable. A perfectly faithful explanation can reveal an indefensible reason.

Interpretable by design: the credit scorecard

The classic banking model is a logistic-regression scorecard, and it remains the benchmark for explainability. The model estimates the log-odds of a good outcome as a sum of feature contributions:

$$ \ln\!\frac{P(\text{good})}{P(\text{bad})} \;=\; \beta_0 + \sum_{j} \beta_j \, x_j. $$

Scorecards convert log-odds to points using a scaling rule: a target score at given odds, and a fixed number of points to double the odds (PDO). With \(\text{Factor} = \text{PDO}/\ln 2\) and \(\text{Offset} = \text{Score}_0 - \text{Factor}\cdot \ln(\text{Odds}_0)\):

$$ \text{Score} = \text{Offset} + \text{Factor}\cdot \ln(\text{odds}) = \underbrace{\text{Offset} + \text{Factor}\,\beta_0}_{\text{base points}} + \sum_j \underbrace{\text{Factor}\,\beta_j x_j}_{\text{points for feature } j}. $$

Example: 600 points at odds of 50:1, with 20 PDO, gives Factor = 28.85 and Offset = 487.1. Every 20 points doubles the odds of repayment.

Because the score is additive, the explanation is exact: each feature contributes a fixed number of points, and the adverse-action reasons are simply the features where the applicant lost the most points relative to a reference (for example the maximum available). Monotonicity is easy to impose and check. Nobody has to approximate anything. This is why Cynthia Rudin's 2019 argument, stop explaining black-box models for high-stakes decisions and use interpretable models instead, is so influential in lending. Modern interpretable methods such as explainable boosting machines (generalised additive models with pairwise interactions), monotonic gradient boosting and sparse rule lists often close most of the accuracy gap.

Shapley values: fair credit for a prediction

When the model is not additive, we need a principled way to split a prediction among the features. Shapley values, from Lloyd Shapley's 1953 work in cooperative game theory, do this. Treat the features as players in a game whose "payout" is the model's prediction. For a coalition \(S\) of features, let \(v(S)\) be the model's expected prediction when only the features in \(S\) are known. The Shapley value of feature \(i\) is its marginal contribution averaged over every order in which features could be added:

$$ \phi_i \;=\; \sum_{S \subseteq N\setminus\{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,\big(n-|S|-1\big)!}{n!}\,\Big[v\big(S\cup\{i\}\big) - v(S)\Big]. $$

It is the unique attribution satisfying four fairness axioms:

  • Efficiency: attributions sum exactly to the prediction minus the baseline, \(\sum_i \phi_i = f(x) - E[f(X)]\).
  • Symmetry: two features that contribute identically get identical credit.
  • Dummy: a feature that never changes the output gets zero.
  • Additivity: for a sum of models, attributions add.
Worked example

Three features, computed by hand

An approval model uses Income (I), credit History (H) and Debt ratio (D). The average approval probability (the baseline, \(v(\varnothing)\)) is 0.50; this applicant's is 0.78. The expected predictions for each coalition are:

v(∅)=0.50 · v(I)=0.60 · v(H)=0.65 · v(D)=0.45 · v(I,H)=0.80 · v(I,D)=0.55 · v(H,D)=0.62 · v(I,H,D)=0.78

Average each feature's marginal contribution over the 3! = 6 orderings:

  • \(\phi_I = \tfrac16(0.10+0.10+0.15+0.16+0.10+0.16) = +0.128\)
  • \(\phi_H = \tfrac16(0.20+0.23+0.15+0.15+0.23+0.17) = +0.188\)
  • \(\phi_D = \tfrac16(-0.02-0.05-0.02-0.03-0.05-0.05) = -0.037\)

Check: \(0.128 + 0.188 - 0.037 = 0.28 = 0.78 - 0.50\). ✓ Credit history is the main reason for approval, and debt ratio slightly reduced it. Note that Income's contribution depends on order (0.10 when added first, 0.16 when added last) because Income and History interact. Shapley averaging settles that ambiguity in a principled way.

What SHAP does not tell you

SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) made Shapley values practical and is now the default explanation tool in industry. Validators need to understand its limits:

  • It is not causal. A large SHAP value means the model relies on the feature, not that changing the feature in the world would change the outcome.
  • Correlated features are handled by assumption. Computing \(v(S)\) means "averaging out" the missing features. Interventional SHAP samples them independently and can create impossible applicants (a £200k salary with a student loan and no credit history). Observational SHAP conditions on the known features and can spread credit onto features the model never uses. The two can give different rankings. The choice should be documented.
  • The baseline matters. “Relative to the average applicant” and “relative to the approval threshold” give different stories. For adverse-action reasons, a reference such as a just-approved applicant is often more meaningful.
  • It can be gamed. Slack et al. (2020) showed a deliberately biased model can be wrapped so that LIME and SHAP explanations hide the bias, because the explainer's synthetic samples are detectable as off-distribution. Explanations are evidence, not proof.
  • Approximations have error. KernelSHAP samples coalitions, while TreeSHAP is exact for tree ensembles. Report which one you used, and its stability.

LIME: local surrogate models

LIME (Ribeiro, Singh & Guestrin, 2016, “Why should I trust you?”) explains one prediction by fitting a simple, interpretable model \(g\) (usually sparse linear) to the black box \(f\) in the neighbourhood of the instance \(x\):

$$ \xi(x) \;=\; \arg\min_{g \in G}\; \mathcal{L}\big(f, g, \pi_x\big) + \Omega(g), $$

\(\mathcal{L}\) measures how badly \(g\) mimics \(f\) on perturbed samples weighted by proximity \(\pi_x\); \(\Omega\) penalises complexity (for example the number of features).

LIME is fast and intuitive, but the explanation depends on the kernel width, the sampling scheme and random seeds, and two nearby applicants can get quite different explanations. The source modules name three general challenges for such surrogate methods: oversimplification can mislead; computation can be costly; and they are ex post, explaining a model rather than constraining it. The ever-present accuracy–interpretability trade-off is the reason to use them at all.

Counterfactual explanations: “what would have changed the outcome?”

Wachter, Mittelstadt and Russell (2017) proposed explaining a decision by the smallest change to the input that would have flipped it: “your application would have been approved if your annual income had been £4,000 higher, or your credit-card utilisation below 35%.” Formally, find the closest point \(x'\) that achieves the desired outcome \(y'\):

$$ x^{*} \;=\; \arg\min_{x'} \;\max_{\lambda}\; \lambda\,\big(f(x') - y'\big)^2 + d(x, x'). $$

Counterfactuals have three attractive properties. They need no access to model internals. They are contrastive, which matches how people actually ask “why?” (Miller, 2019): we want to know why P happened rather than Q. And they are actionable. But a useful counterfactual must respect reality. It must not suggest changing immutable features (age, place of birth) or protected characteristics, and it should be plausible (on the data manifold) and achievable. Ustun, Spangher and Liu (2019) call a feasible counterfactual recourse, and whether each group has comparable recourse is itself a fairness question.

Global explanation tools

  • Permutation feature importance: shuffle one feature's values and measure how much performance drops. Simple and model-agnostic, but misleading with correlated features.
  • Partial dependence (PDP) and individual conditional expectation (ICE) plots: how the prediction changes as one feature varies, on average (PDP) or per individual (ICE). ICE curves that fan out reveal interactions that a PDP hides.
  • Accumulated local effects (ALE): a PDP-like view that behaves better with correlated features.
  • Global surrogates: a shallow tree trained to mimic the black box. Report its fidelity (the \(R^2\) or agreement rate with the original); a low-fidelity surrogate explains nothing.
  • Inverse design (for example LUCID, cited in the source modules): work backwards from an output such as “loan granted” to the distribution of inputs that produce it, exposing the model's internal logic and any unfairness in it.

Explaining large language models

For generative AI, the classical tools are largely inapplicable. There are billions of parameters, no fixed feature set, and outputs that are text rather than a score. Four practical approaches have emerged:

  1. Grounding and citation. Retrieval-augmented systems can show which source passages an answer relied on. This is the most useful form of transparency for enterprise GenAI, and it is testable: does the cited source actually support the claim?
  2. System and model cards describing intended use, training data provenance at a high level, evaluations, known limitations and red-team findings.
  3. Behavioural testing: explaining the system by characterising what it does across thousands of structured test prompts, since we cannot read its mechanism.
  4. Mechanistic interpretability: an active research field (sparse autoencoders, circuit analysis) that tries to identify human-interpretable features inside networks. Promising, but not yet an assurance tool.

A caution: a model's stated “reasoning” (chain of thought) is not guaranteed to be a faithful account of how it produced the answer. It is useful evidence, not an audit trail of the computation.

Transparency is wider than explanation

Recall the three forms of opacity from Chapter 03: secrecy, confidentiality and lack of specialised knowledge. Each needs its own transparency response:

Against secrecy: disclosure

Tell people when AI is used. The EU AI Act Art 50 requires that people be informed when interacting with an AI system, and that synthetic content be marked. Include AI use in privacy notices and customer journeys.

Against confidentiality: documentation

Model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019), datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al., 2018), system cards and technical documentation (EU AI Act Annex IV). For vendor models, contractual access to enough information to do your own risk assessment.

Against lack of knowledge: literacy

Plain-language explanations tested with real customers; AI literacy training for staff (EU AI Act Art 4); accessible transparency reports for the public.

A five-step explanation audit for a credit model

  1. Select a sample of declined applications (say 60, stratified by decline reason and customer segment).
  2. For each, re-generate the explanation from the production model version (reproducibility).
  3. Compare with the reasons actually sent to the customer (consistency).
  4. Perturb inputs within measurement tolerance and check whether the top reasons stay stable (stability).
  5. Check that counterfactual or “what you can do” guidance references only mutable, lawful factors (actionability), and that a human-review route was offered.

Chapter 06

Measuring Model Risk

“All models are wrong, but some are useful.” George Box's aphorism is the founding assumption of model risk management. The job is not to prove a model right, which is impossible. It is to subject the model to serious attempts to prove it wrong, measure how wrong it might be, and keep measuring as the world changes.

In brief

  • Model risk is the potential for adverse consequences from decisions based on incorrect or misused model outputs (SR 11-7). It comes from fundamental error and from misuse.
  • For classifiers, measure discrimination (AUC / Gini, KS), calibration (do predicted probabilities match observed rates?) and stability (PSI) separately. A model can be good at one and poor at another.
  • AI/ML models need more frequent monitoring than traditional models: they have more parameters, less transparency and greater sensitivity to drift.
  • For generative AI, “accuracy” becomes an evaluation programme: curated test sets, rubric or model-based grading, and confidence intervals that tell you how much a pass rate is worth.
  • Every test result is an estimate. Sample size determines how much assurance it gives, and the maths of that is simple enough to put in front of a board.
PractitionerAudit

What is a model, and what is model risk?

SR 11-7 defines a model as “a quantitative method, system, or approach that applies statistical, economic, financial, or mathematical theories, techniques, and assumptions to process input data into quantitative estimates.” The PRA's SS1/23 adopts a similarly broad definition and extends expectations explicitly to AI and machine learning. It also asks firms to govern non-model calculation tools proportionately. Under either definition, a credit-scoring gradient-boosting model is plainly a model. A GenAI chatbot that answers product questions is less clearly so, since it produces text rather than estimates, and firms differ in how they classify it. The prevailing view, which this guide shares, is that any AI system whose output informs a material decision belongs in the model inventory, with the validation approach adjusted to its nature.

The source modules describe any quantitative risk model in terms of three elements: a quantity of interest whose future value is uncertain (a portfolio value in ten days, a borrower's default within a year); a set of potential future scenarios, each weighted by probability; and a risk measure that summarises them (VaR, expected shortfall, a probability of default). Weaknesses can sit in any of the three: incomplete scenario sets (especially rare events), feedback effects (the model's use changes the behaviour it models), and poor communication of results (point estimates reported without their uncertainty).

Overfitting and the bias–variance decomposition

Why never train on the full dataset (one of the source modules' test questions)? Because a model's expected error on new data splits into three parts:

$$ \mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat f(x))^2\big] \;=\; \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}. $$

Flexible ML models reduce bias but can have high variance: they memorise the training data's noise. Performance on the training data then says nothing about performance on new applicants. Holding out data the model never sees (and, for time-series or credit data, holding out a later period) is the basic defence. \(k\)-fold cross-validation averages performance across \(k\) train/test partitions to reduce the luck of a single split. SR 11-7's instruction to analyse “in-sample fit and model performance in holdout samples” remains the most important single test for ML models.

Discrimination: can the model rank risk?

For a scoring model, the ROC curve plots TPR against FPR at every threshold. The area under it (AUC) has a clean probabilistic meaning:

$$ \text{AUC} = P\big(S_{\text{good}} \gt S_{\text{bad}}\big), $$

the probability that a randomly chosen good borrower scores higher than a randomly chosen bad one. An AUC of 0.5 is a coin toss and 1.0 is perfect ranking. Credit risk teams usually quote the Gini coefficient (accuracy ratio), \(\text{Gini} = 2\,\text{AUC} - 1\), and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic, the maximum vertical gap between the cumulative score distributions of goods and bads, \( \text{KS} = \max_s |F_{\text{good}}(s) - F_{\text{bad}}(s)| \). Application scorecards typically achieve Gini coefficients of roughly 0.4–0.7, depending on product and data.

Calibration: do the probabilities mean what they say?

A model can rank perfectly and still be badly calibrated: every score doubled, say, leaves the ranking and the AUC unchanged. Calibration matters whenever probabilities are used as probabilities: pricing, provisioning (IFRS 9 expected credit loss), capital, or fairness (Chapter 04's sufficiency criterion). It is assessed with a reliability diagram (predicted vs observed default rate by score bucket) and summarised by:

$$ \text{Brier score} = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N (s_i - y_i)^2, \qquad \text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{n_b}{N}\,\big|\bar y_b - \bar s_b\big|. $$

The Brier score combines calibration and discrimination; expected calibration error (ECE) isolates calibration by bucket. Traditional tests include Hosmer–Lemeshow and, for PD models, binomial or Jeffreys tests per rating grade.

Stability and drift

Models are trained on the past and applied to the future. When the future stops resembling the past, performance degrades, often without any visible error. There are three kinds of drift:

  • Data (covariate) drift: the input distribution \(P(X)\) changes, for example a marketing campaign brings in younger applicants, or inflation shifts incomes.
  • Concept drift: the relationship \(P(Y\mid X)\) changes, so the same profile now carries different risk (a rate shock changes affordability; fraudsters adapt).
  • Label / prior drift: the base rate \(P(Y)\) changes, for example defaults rise in a downturn.

The workhorse measure of population shift in banking is the Population Stability Index. Bin the score (or a feature) into \(B\) buckets. With \(e_b\) the expected share in bucket \(b\) (development sample) and \(a_b\) the actual share now:

$$ \text{PSI} \;=\; \sum_{b=1}^{B} (a_b - e_b)\,\ln\!\frac{a_b}{e_b}. $$

PSI is a symmetrised Kullback–Leibler divergence, \(D_{KL}(a\|e) + D_{KL}(e\|a)\). Applied to individual features it is often called the characteristic stability index (CSI). Conventional rules of thumb are below 0.10 stable, 0.10–0.25 moderate shift (investigate), and above 0.25 significant shift (act: recalibrate, redevelop or restrict use). These are conventions, not laws. Calibrate your thresholds to the model's sensitivity and to the sampling noise in your bucket counts.

Backtesting: holding models to account against outcomes

Backtesting compares predictions with realised outcomes at a frequency suited to the model's use. The source modules give examples: daily for a trading VaR model, monthly for a credit model, quarterly for asset–liability models. The best-known regime is the Basel traffic-light test for 99% one-day VaR. Over 250 trading days, the expected number of exceptions (losses exceeding VaR) is 2.5. Because exceptions follow a binomial distribution, the zones are set by cumulative probability: green for 0–4 exceptions, amber for 5–9 (capital multiplier add-ons increase), and red for 10 or more (the model is presumed flawed).

Kupiec's proportion-of-failures test formalises this. With \(x\) exceptions in \(T\) observations and target rate \(p\):

$$ LR_{POF} = -2\ln\!\Big[(1-p)^{T-x}p^{x}\Big] + 2\ln\!\Big[\big(1-\tfrac{x}{T}\big)^{T-x}\big(\tfrac{x}{T}\big)^{x}\Big] \;\sim\; \chi^2_1. $$

The same logic applies to AI models whenever outcomes eventually arrive. Does the fraud model's predicted fraud rate match confirmed fraud? Does the PD model's predicted default rate match observed defaults per grade? The traffic-light approach is a useful template for AI monitoring thresholds: set zones from the sampling distribution under the hypothesis that the model is working, so that an amber is genuinely unusual and not noise.

Numerical and implementation risk

A model is a mathematical idea turned into software, and errors can enter in the translation. The source modules separate four numerical issues:

  • Discretisation: continuous processes (time, prices) represented on grids. Step size trades stability against run time. Parameterise it so the effect can be tested.
  • Approximation: replacing hard calculations with convenient ones, such as sensitivity (Taylor) approximations for option prices, or square-root-of-time scaling of VaR, \( \text{VaR}_{h} \approx \sqrt{h}\cdot\text{VaR}_1 \). The latter assumes i.i.d. normal returns, an assumption “often convenient but rarely empirically justified”. Autocorrelation and fat tails break it.
  • Numerical evaluation: integration, optimisation and root-finding with controllable precision. Convergence and stability must be tested, including floating-point rounding.
  • Random numbers: Monte Carlo relies on pseudo-random generators. Use well-tested deterministic generators with recorded seeds so that results can be reproduced.

Implementation is a chain of tasks with different people and incentives. The source modules label the setup tasks M0 (model design, on paper), C0 (core implementation, the calculation engine) and S0 (system implementation: data feeds, interfaces, scheduling, history), and their later adaptations Mt, Ct, St. The core programmer is squeezed between modellers who want maximum accuracy and systems teams who want minimum run time, and so tends to produce optimised, unreadable code full of shortcuts. When the original authors leave, adaptation introduces errors into code nobody fully understands.

The classic bugs (division by zero, arithmetic overflow, buffer overflow, off-by-one loop bounds, runaway recursion) matter less than how bugs show up. A program that crashes regularly is easy to fix. One that crashes occasionally at least signals a problem. One that produces odd-looking results is ambiguous, because the cause could be the model, the data or the code. The dangerous case is the one that produces plausible but wrong results. The London Whale spreadsheet error was of this kind. The defences are independent re-implementation of key calculations, reconciliation, regression testing after every change, and a parallel run before cut-over.

White-box, black-box and the use test

White-box testing examines code, data structures and algorithms line by line; it is more effective. Black-box testing treats the model as closed and probes inputs and outputs; it is less prone to the tester sharing the developer's assumptions, and for proprietary vendor models it is often the only option. The source modules stress that tests should include deliberately unrealistic configurations (missing trades, wrong scales, extreme parameters, data entered by inexperienced users), whatever box the model is in.

The use test is different in kind. It asks whether the model is actually used as intended: accepted, understood, correctly interpreted and applied, with feedback reaching the modellers. It is qualitative, story-based and reported to senior management. For AI it is where you discover automation bias, workarounds, shadow use and misinterpretation.

Common ways results are misread

  • Lack of comprehension of assumptions and limitations.
  • Neglecting model limitations, especially use outside the approved domain. The source modules give the example of an equity allocation model quietly extended to crypto-assets with an ad hoc overlay.
  • Over-emphasis on point estimates without confidence intervals.
  • Ignoring qualitative factors and the context of the decision.
  • Confirmation bias: reading results to fit a preferred conclusion.

How much is a test result worth? The statistics of assurance

Suppose a chatbot passes 300 out of 300 hallucination tests. Can you say the hallucination rate is zero? No. You can say that, with 95% confidence, it is below about 1%. This is the rule of three: with zero failures in \(n\) independent trials, the 95% upper confidence bound on the failure rate is approximately

$$ p_{\text{upper}} \approx \frac{3}{n}, \qquad\text{since } (1-p)^n = 0.05 \;\Rightarrow\; p = 1 - 0.05^{1/n} \approx \frac{-\ln 0.05}{n} = \frac{3.0}{n}. $$

With \(k \gt 0\) observed failures, use an exact (Clopper–Pearson) or Wilson interval. The Wilson score interval for a proportion \(\hat p = k/n\) at \(z = 1.96\) is

$$ \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}. $$

Turn this round and you get the sample size needed to demonstrate a target. To show with 95% confidence that a failure rate is below 0.5%, you need about 600 clean tests. To show it is below 0.1%, you need about 3,000. The same arithmetic drives audit sampling of control operation (Chapter 10), where \( n = \frac{\ln(1-\text{confidence})}{\ln(1-\text{tolerable deviation rate})} \).

Validation: independent, recursive and never finished

Model validation is the independent process of trying to invalidate the model and measuring residual risk. It follows a lifecycle: identification and inventory; initial validation before use (which establishes operational feasibility, documentation to standard, and user training); periodic revalidation scheduled by risk tier (the source modules cite high-risk models every two years, and lower tiers every three or four, alongside annual reviews); change-based validation after material changes; continuous monitoring and backtesting; and finally retirement to a retired-model inventory.

A full validation covers data sources and proxies, assumptions and parameters, limitations, approved products and users, implementation platform, expert judgement and overlays, conceptual soundness and benchmarks, the monitoring plan and its KRIs, documentation quality, back-up plans, implementation and access controls, outcomes analysis, stress and boundary tests, and parameter stability. Four principles from the source modules deserve emphasis:

  • No “valid” model: validation is a series of attempts to invalidate. Passing means the model has withstood rigorous testing so far.
  • Usefulness over validity: an imperfect model with known, managed limitations can be approved with restrictions.
  • Recursive validation: the validation itself must be open to challenge. Does it rely on defective data? Does it stay within the intended domain?
  • Effective challenge needs independence, competence and influence. A validator who is always overruled is not a control.

How AI/ML changes the review framework

The source modules identify three differences for AI/ML:

  1. Frequency. SR 11-7's “at least annually” review is often too slow. More parameters, less transparency and higher sensitivity to drift justify monitoring dashboards and trigger-based reviews driven by input changes, KPIs (including bias indicators) and business volume.
  2. Stakeholders. AI decisions touch HR, compliance, data protection and operational risk, so these functions should be involved from development onwards. Approval bodies need enough AI literacy not to reject good models, or wave through bad ones, out of incomprehension.
  3. Content. For black-box models: explainability testing, benchmarking against classical challengers, holdout and out-of-time testing, analysis of specific borderline cases (applicants just either side of the cut-off), k-fold approaches where classical sensitivity analysis breaks down, and review of output reporting for signs of bias.
Assurance angle

Re-performance beats inspection

Internal audit's role is “validation of the validation”: testing that the model risk framework is designed and operating effectively, not re-validating every model. The most persuasive audit evidence is re-performance. Recompute a sample of the validator's key metrics (AUC, PSI, calibration by grade, fairness ratios) from source data and confirm they match. Check that monitoring breaches actually led to the escalation the policy requires.

Chapter 07

Frameworks, Law & Standards

The global AI rulebook has four layers: international principles that set direction, laws that set obligations, standards that describe how to comply, and sector guidance that tells regulated firms what supervisors expect. Jurisdictions differ sharply on the second layer and agree remarkably on the other three.

In brief

  • EU: a comprehensive, risk-tiered horizontal law. Prohibitions apply from Feb 2025 and GPAI obligations from Aug 2025. Annex III high-risk obligations (including credit scoring) now apply from 2 Dec 2027 after the July 2026 Digital Omnibus; AI in regulated products from 2 Aug 2028.
  • UK: no AI Act. Five cross-sector principles are applied by existing regulators. In financial services the FCA has again (July 2026, Mills Review) declined AI-specific rules and relies on the Consumer Duty and SM&CR. The PRA's SS1/23 governs model risk, including AI.
  • US: no federal AI statute. Existing law (fair lending, FTC Act, employment) plus a growing patchwork of state laws, with federal policy tilted towards deregulation since 2025. Private litigation fills the gap.
  • Standards are converging: NIST AI RMF (structure), ISO/IEC 42001 (certifiable management system) and ISO/IEC 23894 (risk management) give a jurisdiction-neutral operating model that maps onto every regime.
  • For a multinational, the practical strategy is one global control framework built to the strictest common standard, with jurisdictional overlays.

Layer 2a: The European Union — the AI Act in depth

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act, is the world's first comprehensive horizontal AI law. It entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. Like the GDPR, it has extraterritorial reach: it applies to providers placing AI systems on the EU market and to deployers in the EU, and also where the output of an AI system is used in the EU. A UK or US bank scoring EU-resident applicants is potentially in scope.

Roles

Obligations attach to roles, not to organisations. The provider develops an AI system (or has it developed) and places it on the market under its own name. The deployer uses it under its authority. Importers, distributors and authorised representatives have supply-chain duties. A bank that builds its own credit model is both provider and deployer. A bank that buys a vendor's model is a deployer, but becomes a provider if it substantially modifies the system or puts its own name on it. Correctly mapping roles for every system in the inventory is one of the first compliance tasks.

The risk pyramid

Prohibited practices (Article 5)

From 2 February 2025 the following are banned: (1) subliminal, manipulative or deceptive techniques that materially distort behaviour and cause significant harm; (2) exploitation of vulnerabilities due to age, disability or social or economic situation; (3) social scoring leading to detrimental or disproportionate treatment; (4) predicting an individual's risk of committing a crime based solely on profiling or personality traits; (5) untargeted scraping of facial images to build recognition databases; (6) emotion recognition in workplaces and education (except for medical or safety reasons); (7) biometric categorisation to infer sensitive characteristics such as race, political opinion, religion or sexual orientation; and (8) real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement, with narrow exceptions. The 2026 Digital Omnibus added a prohibition on AI “nudifier” tools and systems generating child sexual abuse material.

For banks, items (1) to (3) matter most. Collections, retention and cross-sell journeys that exploit financial vulnerability, or scoring that uses social behaviour unrelated to credit, could come close to the line.

High-risk systems

A system is high-risk if it is (a) a safety component of, or itself, a product under the EU harmonisation legislation in Annex I (machinery, medical devices, toys, vehicles and so on), or (b) used in one of the Annex III areas: biometrics; critical infrastructure; education; employment and worker management; access to essential private and public services, which explicitly includes creditworthiness assessment and credit scoring of natural persons (except fraud detection) and risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance; law enforcement; migration and border control; and administration of justice and democratic processes. Article 6(3) allows an Annex III system to escape high-risk status where it performs only a narrow procedural or preparatory task and does not materially influence decisions, except that any system that profiles natural persons is always high-risk.

Transparency obligations (Article 50)

Regardless of risk tier, people must be told when they are interacting with an AI system unless it is obvious. Providers of generative systems must mark synthetic audio, image, video and text outputs in a machine-readable way. Deployers of emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation systems must inform people exposed to them. Deep fakes must be disclosed. According to the September 2026 briefing, these transparency obligations were not broadly deferred by the Omnibus.

General-purpose AI models (Articles 51–55)

Since 2 August 2025, providers of general-purpose AI models must maintain technical documentation, supply information to downstream providers, have a copyright policy, and publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training content. Models with systemic risk (presumed above \(10^{25}\) FLOPs of training compute, or designated by the Commission) must also carry out model evaluations including adversarial testing, assess and mitigate systemic risks, report serious incidents and ensure cybersecurity. A GPAI Code of Practice published in 2025 is the main route to demonstrating compliance. For a bank these are mainly supplier obligations, but they shape what information you can contractually demand from your foundation-model provider.

Penalties

Up to €35m or 7% of worldwide annual turnover (whichever is higher) for prohibited practices; up to €15m or 3% for most other obligations; up to €7.5m or 1% for supplying incorrect or misleading information to authorities. (Early drafts cited in some training materials used 1.5% for the lowest tier; the final text uses 1%.) Lower caps apply to SMEs.

Governance and supervision

The AI Office within the European Commission supervises GPAI models and coordinates enforcement. The AI Board of member-state representatives advises. National market surveillance authorities enforce for AI systems, and for AI used by regulated financial institutions this role generally falls to the financial supervisors, integrating AI Act supervision with prudential and conduct supervision.

The wider EU digital rulebook

  • GDPR (2018): lawful basis, purpose limitation, minimisation, data subject rights, DPIAs, and Article 22 on solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects. Breach notification within 72 hours. Extraterritorial, and the single biggest influence on global privacy practice.
  • DORA (Digital Operational Resilience Act, applicable January 2025): ICT risk management, incident reporting, resilience testing and ICT third-party risk for financial entities, including oversight of critical ICT third-party providers. AI and cloud model providers fall squarely within its third-party provisions.
  • Digital Services Act and Digital Markets Act (2022): platform duties on illegal content, recommender transparency (including a non-profiling option on very large platforms), and gatekeeper obligations against self-preferencing.
  • Revised Product Liability Directive (2024): explicitly treats software, including AI, as a product, with strict liability for defects. It applies to products placed on the market from December 2026. The separate AI Liability Directive proposal was withdrawn in 2025.
  • EBA Guidelines on loan origination and monitoring (2020): expect institutions using automated models in creditworthiness assessment to understand, document and explain them, and to manage bias. This is a prudential hook that pre-dates the AI Act.

Layer 2b: The United Kingdom — principles through existing regulators

The UK chose not to legislate horizontally. The 2023 white paper, A pro-innovation approach to AI regulation, set five cross-sector principles for existing regulators to interpret and apply within their remits:

1. Safety, security & robustness

AI functions securely and as intended across its lifecycle, with risks continually identified and managed.

2. Transparency & explainability

Appropriate information about the system and its decisions, proportionate to the risk.

3. Fairness

No undermining of legal rights, unfair discrimination or unfair market outcomes.

4. Accountability & governance

Effective oversight with clear lines of accountability across the lifecycle.

5. Contestability & redress

Affected parties can contest harmful outcomes or decisions.

Institutions

The AI Security Institute (renamed from AI Safety Institute in 2025) evaluates frontier models. The Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum (CMA, FCA, ICO, Ofcom) coordinates the regulators.

UK financial services: the rules that already apply

For a UK bank, the practical centre of gravity is not a new AI rulebook but existing duties applied to AI. The FCA's 2024 AI update mapped each white-paper principle onto its existing rules, and the July 2026 Mills Review confirmed the approach. It identified four AI-driven shifts to 2030 (transformed firm operations, agent-led consumer journeys, changes to competition and market power, and amplified fraud and cyber risk) but recommended no AI-specific regulation. Open questions remain, notably what “reasonable steps” a senior manager must take to avoid AI-caused harm. The Treasury Select Committee has asked the FCA for guidance on this by the end of 2026. The FCA also runs an AI Lab, a Supercharged Sandbox and AI Live Testing, and plans a publication on AI good and poor practice.

Layer 2c: The United States — sector law and a state patchwork

The US has no federal AI statute. The 2023 Executive Order on safe, secure and trustworthy AI was revoked in January 2025, and federal policy since then, including the July 2025 AI Action Plan, has prioritised innovation and reduced federal pursuit of disparate-impact theories. That raises the relative importance of three other channels:

  • Existing sector law. ECOA/Regulation B adverse-action requirements (with CFPB circulars in 2022 and 2023 confirming that complex algorithms are no excuse for vague reasons); the Fair Housing Act; the FTC Act's prohibition on unfair or deceptive practices, used against Rite Aid (facial recognition) and to order algorithmic disgorgement, the deletion of models trained on improperly obtained data (Everalbum, Kurbo/WW); employment discrimination law; and banking model-risk guidance (SR 11-7/OCC 2011-12 and the OCC's 2021 model risk handbook).
  • State law. New York City's Local Law 144 (bias audits of automated employment decision tools, enforced since 2023); Colorado's AI Act (duties of care for high-risk AI developers and deployers, with its effective date pushed back to mid-2026); Illinois rules on AI in employment decisions; California's privacy-agency rules on automated decision-making technology and its 2025 frontier-model transparency law; and a growing set of disclosure laws. A proposed federal moratorium on state AI laws was dropped in 2025, though pressure for federal pre-emption continues.
  • Private litigation. Mobley v. Workday (a vendor potentially liable as an employer's agent), consumer chatbot cases, and copyright suits against model developers.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary but has become the de facto US, and in practice global, structure for AI risk programmes (see Layer 3).

Frontier safety frameworks

Developers of the most capable models publish their own safety frameworks: Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework and Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework. Each defines capability thresholds (for example meaningful uplift to biological or cyber attack, or autonomous replication) that trigger escalating security and deployment safeguards, with pre-deployment testing by internal teams and by government institutes such as the UK AI Security Institute. For an enterprise buyer these frameworks are part of supplier due diligence: they show how a provider will behave when its model's capabilities change.

Layer 4: Assurance guidance

  • Institute of Internal Auditors: the Global Internal Audit Standards (effective January 2025), the Three Lines Model (2020) and the IIA's AI Auditing Framework.
  • ISAE 3000 (Revised) and ISAE 3402 / SOC 2: the bases for external assurance reports on non-financial subject matter and service-organisation controls, increasingly used for AI.
  • UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology: the AI assurance roadmap and techniques portfolio, and a developing market for third-party AI assurance.
  • Bank of England / FCA AI surveys and the AI Public–Private Forum: benchmarks on adoption and emerging risks across UK financial services.

Designing for regulatory divergence

A global institution faces an EU with detailed statutory obligations, a UK with principles applied through conduct and prudential rules, a US where litigation and state law set the pace, and APAC regulators with their own sector guidance. The workable design is: (1) a single global AI policy and control framework built to the strictest common denominator (which in practice means the EU AI Act's high-risk requirements plus SS1/23-style model risk management); (2) jurisdictional overlays for specific items (EU conformity assessment and registration, FRIA, NYC bias audits, UK Consumer Duty outcome reporting); and (3) a regulatory-change process that updates the mappings as rules move, as they did in July 2026.

Chapter 08

Implementing Responsible AI in the Enterprise

Publishing values is easy; embedding them is hard. The organisations that close the gap between principles and practice share a recognisable operating model: clear ownership, a complete inventory, proportionate tiering, lifecycle gates with teeth, and a culture in which raising a concern is normal.

In brief

  • Build responsible AI into the existing risk architecture (three lines, model risk, data protection, conduct, third-party) rather than as a parallel ethics structure.
  • Eight building blocks: inventory, risk tiering, impact assessment, documentation, testing and red-teaming, monitoring and incident response, third-party management, and governance and culture.
  • The AI inventory is the foundation, and it is almost always incomplete: most organisations underestimate the AI embedded in vendor software and in staff use of GenAI tools.
  • Tiering decides everything downstream. It must be simple enough to apply consistently, with non-negotiable overrides for prohibited and legally high-risk uses.
  • The second line must have decision rights, including the right to block or pause, not just the right to advise.

Governance structure

A typical mature structure in a large bank looks like the diagram below. The detail varies, but the design principles do not: a board-level owner of AI risk appetite; an executive forum with decision authority; an independent second-line function that sets standards and challenges; accountable first-line owners for every system; and an internal audit function with the skills to test all of it.

The three lines, applied to AI

The IIA's Three Lines Model (2020) describes roles rather than rigid boxes. For AI:

  • First line: product, engineering and business owners own AI risk day to day. They register systems, perform impact assessments, build and test to standard, operate human oversight, monitor performance and fix issues. A first-line risk and control team (sometimes called "line 1.5") helps them do this consistently.
  • Second line: the Responsible AI office, model risk management, compliance, privacy, conduct risk, operational and third-party risk set policy and standards, independently validate higher-tier systems, challenge first-line assessments, aggregate the AI risk profile and report against appetite.
  • Third line: internal audit gives independent assurance to the board that the framework is designed and operating effectively, and that the second line's challenge is real.

The two most common structural failures

1. An advisory-only second line. If the Responsible AI office can recommend but not require, block or escalate with consequence, the first line will eventually overrule it under deadline pressure. Give it defined decision rights: sign-off for Tier 1 and 2 systems, the right to impose conditions, and a direct escalation route to the CRO and board risk committee.

2. A third line without technical capability. Internal audit teams that cannot read model documentation, query data or re-perform a fairness test end up auditing the process about the models instead of the models. Build or buy data-science audit capability, and secure access rights to model artefacts and environments.

Accountability: every system has a name against it

Accountability is the principle most often cited and least often implemented. In practice it means each AI system in the inventory has a named business owner (accountable for use and outcomes), a model owner (accountable for performance and maintenance, often the same person), and a technical owner (accountable for the build). Every system rolls up to a senior manager under SM&CR or its equivalent. The source modules recommend giving model ownership to the business users of the output. The lifecycle, with gates that bite

Controls become real when they are attached to gates: points in the lifecycle where a system cannot proceed without specified evidence and approval. Gates should be proportionate. A Tier 4 internal productivity tool passes the intake gate almost automatically, while a Tier 1 credit model faces every gate in full.

Building block 1: the AI inventory

You cannot govern what you cannot see. The inventory is the single most important control, and the one most often found incomplete. It should capture everything built, bought or embedded: in-house models; vendor systems; AI features switched on inside SaaS platforms (CRM, HR, contact-centre, productivity suites); foundation-model APIs; and, at least at the level of approved tools, staff use of general-purpose GenAI.

Beyond the traditional model-inventory fields (owner, purpose, methodology, vendor/in-house, use and frequency, restrictions, materiality, assumptions and weaknesses, documentation, interdependencies, validation history, monitoring, regulatory use), the source modules recommend AI-specific attributes: complexity of methodology (for example layers and parameters), data usage (volume, structure, quality, unlabelled or unstructured data), output type (supervised with a measurable target, or unsupervised with no direct accuracy test), recalibration regime (static, periodically retrained, or continuously learning), and ethical attributes (bias exposure, explainability tier). To these, a 2026 inventory adds EU AI Act role and risk classification, whether the system is customer-facing, degree of autonomy (advisory, human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, fully automated), and foundation-model dependency.

Finding the AI you don't know about

  • Procurement hooks: an AI question in every new-supplier and contract-renewal workflow.
  • Vendor attestation: annual questionnaires to existing critical suppliers asking whether AI features exist, are enabled, or have been added.
  • Technical discovery: network and SaaS-usage scanning for AI service endpoints; code-repository scans for ML libraries and model-API calls; cloud-resource tagging.
  • Expense and card data: subscriptions to AI tools on corporate cards are a classic shadow-AI signal.
  • Periodic attestation by business heads that their inventory entries are complete, which makes an omission an accountability matter.
Assurance angle

Testing inventory completeness

Completeness cannot be tested by sampling the inventory. You have to sample from outside it. Take independent populations (the supplier master file, cloud billing lines, API gateway logs, code repositories, expense claims), identify AI-indicative items, and trace each back to the inventory. The proportion not found is a direct measure of completeness. Findings here are among the most frequent and most important in AI audits.

Building block 2: risk tiering

Tiering makes governance proportionate. It decides how much documentation, testing, validation, approval and monitoring each system receives. The source modules name the core model-risk factors as materiality, complexity and exposure, noting that complexity can raise a tier even where materiality is low, and that some minimum assessment applies to every model. For AI, add impact on people, autonomy and data sensitivity. The rule that matters most is that certain conditions override the score: a prohibited use stops the process, and an EU AI Act high-risk or customer credit decision is always at least Tier 1 or 2, whatever the arithmetic says.

Building block 3: impact assessment

An AI impact assessment (AIIA) is a structured, pre-deployment review of purpose, data, affected groups, potential harms and mitigations. It is analogous to a DPIA, and ideally integrated with the DPIA, the EU fundamental rights impact assessment where required, and any equality impact assessment, so that teams complete one coherent assessment rather than four overlapping ones. ISO/IEC 42005 (2025) now provides a standard structure. A good AIIA answers:

  1. Purpose and necessity: what problem, for whom, and why AI? What is the non-AI alternative?
  2. Affected people: who is affected directly and indirectly; are vulnerable groups present; how significant is the effect on them?
  3. Data: sources, lawful basis, provenance and legal right to use (the source modules warn that the FTC has ordered models trained on improperly obtained data to be destroyed), representativeness, protected attributes and proxies.
  4. Harms: fairness, safety, privacy, autonomy and manipulation, explainability, security, and foreseeable misuse. Rate likelihood and severity.
  5. Human oversight design: who reviews what, with what information, authority and time.
  6. Transparency and contestability: how people are told, how they get reasons, and how they challenge.
  7. Mitigations and residual risk: what controls are applied and what risk remains; who accepts it.
  8. Monitoring plan: metrics, thresholds, frequency and escalation.

Building block 4: documentation

The SR 11-7 test for documentation remains the best: detailed enough that someone unfamiliar with the model can understand how it operates, its limitations and its key assumptions. For AI the standard artefacts are the model card (intended use, out-of-scope uses, performance overall and by subgroup, evaluation data, ethical considerations, caveats), the datasheet for each training dataset (motivation, composition, collection process, preprocessing, uses, distribution, maintenance), and for GenAI and agentic systems a system card covering the full system: model, retrieval sources, tools and permissions, guardrails, evaluation and red-team results. For EU high-risk systems, Annex IV sets the statutory contents.

Building block 5: testing and red-teaming

Pre-deployment testing covers performance, robustness, fairness and explainability (Chapters 04–06). For generative systems, add red-teaming: structured adversarial testing by people and tools trying to make the system fail through jailbreaks, prompt injection, data exfiltration, harmful or non-compliant content, hallucinated commitments and manipulation of vulnerable users. Effective red teams are independent of the build team, work from a threat model, record every attempt (not just the successes), rate findings by severity, and re-test after fixes. Their attack library then becomes a regression suite that runs on every model or prompt change.

Building block 6: monitoring and incident response

Production monitoring watches for performance decay, drift (PSI and CSI), fairness breaches, override-rate anomalies, complaint spikes, guardrail triggers and, for GenAI, sampled output quality. Each metric needs a threshold, an owner and an action. An AI incident should be defined, for example "an event in which an AI system caused or could plausibly have caused harm to customers, the firm or third parties, or breached law or policy". Incidents need a classification scheme, an escalation path aligned to operational-incident processes, root-cause analysis, and, critically, the authority and technical means to pause or withdraw a system (a kill switch with a tested fallback process).

Building block 7: third-party AI risk

“You own your vendors' AI” is the clearest lesson of the case law. Third-party management needs AI-specific extensions:

Due diligence questions

  • What AI is in the product, and is it on by default?
  • Training data sources and legal right to use them; personal data involved?
  • Performance and fairness test results on populations like ours; can we test independently?
  • Explainability available to us and to our customers?
  • Human oversight features; logging; audit trail?
  • Security testing, red-team results, incident history?
  • Certifications (ISO/IEC 42001, 27001, SOC 2) and scope?
  • Foundation models used downstream, and their providers' safety frameworks?

Contract clauses

  • Audit and information rights, including for regulators.
  • Testing data and access to validate on our population.
  • Change notification: advance notice of model updates, retraining and deprecation; version pinning.
  • Data use restrictions: no training on our data without consent; location; retention; deletion.
  • Incident notification within defined hours.
  • Indemnities for IP infringement and unlawfully sourced training data.
  • Sub-processor transparency and approval.
  • Exit and portability: transition assistance; data and artefact return.

Concentration and exit risk deserve board attention. If several critical processes depend on one foundation-model provider, a provider outage, a policy change or a capability withdrawal (the 2026 Anthropic–DoD dispute showed that a government can move against a provider suddenly) becomes a business-continuity event. Mitigations include multi-provider architectures, abstraction layers, tested fallbacks to a simpler model or a manual process, and exit plans that have actually been rehearsed.

Building block 8: governance, people and culture

Structures fail without the people and culture to operate them.

  • AI literacy. Since February 2025 the EU AI Act (Article 4) has required providers and deployers to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff dealing with AI systems. Build a role-based curriculum: all staff (acceptable use, recognising AI output, data handling); users of AI decisions (limitations, automation bias, when to override); builders (fairness, documentation, secure development); validators and auditors (technical testing); senior managers and the board (risk, accountability, the questions to ask).
  • Incentives. If product teams are measured only on speed and revenue, they will treat governance as friction. Put responsible-AI objectives in scorecards and reward the delayed launch that avoided harm.
  • Speak-up. Staff must be able to raise AI concerns, anonymously if needed, with protection from retaliation. The departures from ethics teams in 2020–23 are a warning about what happens when dissent is costly.
  • Diversity of perspective. Diverse build and review teams catch harms that homogeneous teams do not see.
  • Tone from the top. The source modules put the board's role simply: approve the framework, oversee compliance and risk, and set the tone that makes data and AI governance part of how the firm works.

Chapter 09

The Control Library

A principle is a hope; a control is a mechanism. This chapter converts the responsible-AI framework into specific, testable controls, each with an objective, an owner, a frequency, evidence and a way to prove it works, and maps them to the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF, PRA SS1/23 and the Consumer Duty.

In brief

  • Every control answers: who does what, when, to what standard, leaving what evidence, and what happens when an exception is found.
  • Layer controls along each failure pathway: preventive (stop it happening), detective (notice quickly) and corrective (limit the damage and fix it).
  • AI needs more automated and continuous controls than traditional models, because retraining, prompt changes and vendor updates happen far more often than annual reviews.
  • The 42 specimen controls below are a starting library. Tailor them to your risk profile, tiering and existing control framework rather than adopting them wholesale.

The anatomy of a well-designed control

Most control weaknesses are visible in the control description before any testing. Compare:

Weak description

“AI models are monitored for bias.”

Who monitors? Which models? Which metric, and what threshold? How often? What happens on a breach? What evidence is kept? This control cannot be tested, because nothing in it can fail.

Strong description

MON-02: Fairness outcome monitoring

Each month, the model owner for every Tier 1–2 customer-decisioning model reviews the automated fairness report showing the adverse-impact ratio and TPR gap for each monitored group, with 95% confidence intervals. Where AIR < 0.80 or the TPR gap exceeds 5 points for two consecutive months, the owner raises an issue in the risk system within 5 working days and notifies Model Risk. Evidence: signed-off report, issue log.

The strong version specifies the performer, frequency, population, precision (what exactly is compared to what), the threshold for investigation, the action on exceptions, and the evidence. Those are also exactly the attributes an auditor tests.

Layering controls along a failure pathway

Risks become incidents along causal pathways. The bow-tie is a useful way to design controls for a pathway. On the left are threats and the preventive barriers against them; in the middle is the top event; on the right are consequences and the mitigating (detective and corrective) barriers. A robust design has several independent barriers on each side, so that no single failure causes harm.

INV-01

AI inventory completeness reconciliation

Detective · IT-dependent manual · Quarterly
Objective

All AI systems built, bought or embedded are recorded in the AI inventory with complete, accurate attributes, so that no system escapes tiering and governance.

Risk addressed

Ungoverned AI (shadow AI, vendor-embedded features, informal models) causes harm without assessment, testing or oversight; the firm cannot report its true AI risk profile.

How it works
  • Quarterly, the RAI Office extracts four independent populations: new and renewed suppliers, cloud and SaaS AI endpoints from gateway logs, repositories with ML libraries or model-API calls, and AI-related expense lines.
  • An automated match flags items not linked to an inventory ID.
  • Each unmatched item is triaged within 10 working days: registered, confirmed non-AI, or escalated as a policy breach.
  • Business heads attest completeness of their inventory segment.
Evidence
  • Source extracts with run date and parameters
  • Reconciliation output and triage log
  • Signed attestations
  • Completeness KPI trend
Test of design

Are the source populations sufficient to catch the main routes by which AI enters (build, buy, embed, staff tools)? Are match rules sensible? Is triage timely and independent of the owners concerned?

Test of operating effectiveness

For 2 of 4 quarters, re-perform the reconciliation from source; sample 25 unmatched items and verify triage outcome and timeliness; independently sample 30 items from a population the control does not use (such as contract repositories) to estimate residual incompleteness.

KRI

% of discovered AI items not in inventory at discovery (target < 5%); median days to register.

ISO 42001 A.4, A.6 · NIST GOVERN 1.6, MAP 1 · SS1/23 P1 · EU AIA Art 26 (knowing what you deploy)

DEV-03

Pre-deployment fairness testing

Preventive · Manual with tooling · Per release
Objective

Customer-affecting AI systems do not produce unjustified disparities across protected groups before they are deployed or materially changed.

Risk addressed

Indirect discrimination (Equality Act, fair lending), Consumer Duty poor outcomes, EU AIA Art 10 bias obligations, reputational harm.

How it works
  • The impact assessment records the primary fairness criterion, monitored groups and tolerances, approved by the RAI Office.
  • Developers run the standard fairness test pack on a held-out, out-of-time sample: AIR, TPR/FPR gaps, calibration by group, and intersectional cuts where n ≥ 100, all with confidence intervals.
  • Proxy analysis of the feature set (a proxy model's AUC for predicting the protected attribute) and a documented search for less discriminatory alternatives.
  • Breaches of tolerance require mitigation or a documented justification accepted by the Executive AI Committee.
Evidence
  • Approved fairness criteria and tolerances
  • Test pack output (versioned, reproducible)
  • LDA search memo
  • Committee decision on any residual disparity
Test of design

Is the chosen criterion appropriate to the use case (Chapter 04)? Are tolerances set before results are seen? Are protected-attribute data sourced lawfully and accurately enough? Does the pack include statistical significance?

Test of operating effectiveness

Select all Tier 1 and a sample of Tier 2 releases in the period (for example 15). For each, confirm criteria were approved before testing; re-perform key metrics from the validation dataset; confirm breaches were mitigated or formally accepted.

KRI

Number of Tier 1–2 systems in production without a current fairness test; releases with unresolved tolerance breaches.

Maps to

EU AIA Arts 9, 10(2)(f)–(g), 10(5) · ISO 42001 A.5, A.7 · NIST MEASURE 2.11 · Consumer Duty (outcomes by group) · Equality Act s.19

VAL-01

Independent validation before use

Preventive · Manual · Per model & periodic
Objective

Tier 1–2 AI systems are effectively challenged by competent, independent validators before first use, after material change and periodically, and are used only within approved conditions.

Risk addressed

Fundamental model error and misuse; the absence of effective challenge (SR 11-7, SS1/23 P4).

How it works
  • Model Risk validators, organisationally independent of development, assess conceptual soundness, data, implementation, performance, fairness, explainability, robustness and the monitoring plan against the validation standard.
  • Findings are rated (critical, high, medium, low). Critical findings block use; high findings require conditions or remediation dates.
  • The validation report states approved uses, restrictions and the monitoring regime, all recorded in the inventory.
  • Revalidation frequency by tier (for example Tier 1 every two years, with annual review), plus trigger-based reviews.
Evidence
  • Validation report, findings log and rating
  • Independence confirmation
  • Inventory record of conditions
  • Revalidation schedule vs actual
Test of design

Is independence structural (reporting line, remuneration)? Do validators have AI/ML competence? Does the standard cover AI-specific content (explainability, fairness, GenAI evaluation)? Can findings actually block deployment?

Test of operating effectiveness

Sample 10–15 validations; assess depth against the standard; re-perform selected tests; confirm no model went live before validation or with open critical findings; check overdue revalidations against the schedule.

KRI

Overdue validations (count and % of Tier 1–2); models in use with open high or critical findings; average finding age.

Maps to

SS1/23 P4 · SR 11-7 · EU AIA Arts 9, 15, 17, 43 · ISO 42001 A.6 (verification & validation) · NIST MEASURE

DEP-01

Human oversight design and override logging

Preventive & detective · IT-dependent manual · Continuous
Objective

Where a human is required in or on the loop, oversight is meaningful: reviewers have the information, competence, authority and time to disagree with the AI, and their interventions are recorded.

Risk addressed

Automation bias and rubber-stamping; decisions that are "solely automated" in substance while appearing human-reviewed (GDPR Art 22); EU AIA Art 14 failures.

How it works
  • The oversight design records what the reviewer sees (score, reasons, confidence, known limitations), what they can do (accept, override, refer) and the expected review time.
  • The system captures every recommendation, the human decision, the reason for any override, and the time taken.
  • A monthly override-rate report by reviewer and team is compared with an expected band (for example 3–20%); outliers are investigated.
  • Reviewers complete model-specific training, including on automation bias.
Evidence
  • Oversight design document
  • Decision and override logs
  • Monthly override analysis
  • Training completion records
Test of design

Could a reviewer realistically disagree? Do they get information beyond the model output? Is the time budget sufficient? Is override frictionless, or penalised?

Test of operating effectiveness

Analyse full-population logs: override-rate distribution, time-to-decision, and reviewers with near-zero overrides. Sample 25 accepted and 25 overridden cases and assess whether the review was substantive.

KRI

Override rate (two-sided band); % of reviews below minimum time; untrained reviewers.

Maps to

EU AIA Arts 14, 26(2) · UK GDPR Art 22 / DUAA safeguards · ISO 42001 A.9 · NIST MANAGE 2 · Consumer Duty

MON-03

GenAI output quality-assurance sampling

Detective · Manual + automated · Weekly
Objective

Customer-facing and decision-support GenAI outputs remain accurate, grounded, compliant and appropriate in production, and degradation is detected quickly.

Risk addressed

Hallucinated or wrong information (Air Canada, Deloitte), non-compliant statements, harmful responses to vulnerable customers, silent degradation after model or prompt changes.

How it works
  • An automated grader scores 100% of conversations for groundedness, policy terms and vulnerability signals, flagging high-risk items.
  • Weekly, trained QA reviewers grade a stratified random sample (for example 200 conversations, over-weighting flagged items and complaints) against the rubric; the automated grader is recalibrated against human scores (kappa ≥ 0.8).
  • Results are reported with Wilson confidence intervals against tolerances; a breach triggers root-cause analysis and, if severe, rollback or kill switch.
Evidence
  • Sampling plan and population counts
  • Graded samples with reviewer IDs
  • Weekly QA dashboard; kappa calibration
  • Breach tickets and actions
Test of design

Is the sample large enough to detect the tolerance (Chapter 06)? Is sampling random within strata, and independent of the build team? Does the rubric cover conduct as well as accuracy?

Test of operating effectiveness

For 6 sampled weeks, re-draw the sample from the population to confirm randomness; re-grade 20 conversations independently and compare; trace breaches to action.

KRI

Hallucination rate (upper 95% bound); % of vulnerability signals not escalated; grader–human kappa.

Maps to

EU AIA Arts 15, 26(5), 72 · NIST AI 600-1 (confabulation) · ISO 42001 A.6 (operation & monitoring) · Consumer Duty (consumer understanding, support)

TPR-03

Foundation-model change regression testing

Preventive · Automated · Per provider change
Objective

Changes to third-party foundation models (new versions, deprecations, silent updates) do not degrade dependent systems without detection and approval.

Risk addressed

Behavioural drift after provider updates; loss of guardrail effectiveness; unplanned deprecation; concentration risk.

How it works
  • Production uses pinned model versions where the provider allows.
  • A provider change notice or scheduled deprecation triggers the full evaluation and red-team regression suite in the pipeline against the new version.
  • Automated comparison against baseline tolerances; any degradation beyond tolerance blocks promotion.
  • A canary release to a small share of traffic with enhanced monitoring before full cut-over.
Evidence
  • Version register per system
  • Pipeline run logs and comparison reports
  • Promotion approvals
  • Canary monitoring results
Test of design

Is the evaluation suite representative and current? Can unpinned or silent updates occur? Are tolerances defined? Is there a fallback if the new version fails?

Test of operating effectiveness

For every provider version change in the period, verify the suite ran before promotion, results were within tolerance or formally accepted, and the canary stage occurred.

KRI

Production calls to unpinned models; version changes promoted without a full regression run; days to deprecation for each system.

Maps to

ISO 42001 A.10 · NIST MANAGE 3 (third-party) · DORA ICT third-party · PRA SS2/21 · EU AIA Art 25 (value chain)

Automating controls: governance as code

The source modules observe that with growing volumes of AI models, more frequent retraining and a greater impact of errors, automated ongoing controls, where practical and responsible, become more relevant. Modern ML platforms let many controls be enforced in the deployment pipeline itself:

  • Registry gates: a model cannot be promoted to production unless its inventory ID, tier, approved validation and model card exist in the registry.
  • Test gates: performance, fairness and evaluation suites run automatically; failure against approved tolerances blocks deployment.
  • Lineage capture: every production model links automatically to the exact code commit, training data snapshot and hyperparameters (reproducibility, DEV-07).
  • Continuous monitoring: drift, fairness and output quality computed on schedule, with alerts routed to owners and issues opened automatically.
  • Policy engines for GenAI: input and output filters, PII redaction and tool-permission checks enforced at the gateway.

Automated controls change the assurance approach. Instead of sampling many instances of a manual control, the auditor tests the configuration and change management of the automated control once (does the gate do what it claims, and who can change or bypass it?), then relies on it for the whole population. This “test one, rely on all” logic, standard in IT general controls, makes automation valuable for both control quality and assurance efficiency.

Common control-design deficiencies in AI programmes

  • Policy without procedure: “models must be fair” with no metric, threshold or owner.
  • Gates that can be skipped: the approval step exists in the process document but not in the deployment tooling.
  • Monitoring without action: dashboards exist; nobody is obliged to look, or to act on red.
  • Vendor blind spots: controls designed for in-house models that cannot operate on black-box vendor systems.
  • Tolerances set after results: fairness or accuracy thresholds chosen to fit the model's performance.
  • Paper human oversight: a named reviewer who approves hundreds of cases an hour.

Assurance: Proving It Works

A control nobody tests is a hope with paperwork. Assurance is the discipline of gathering enough reliable evidence to tell a board, a regulator or the public, with stated confidence, that AI risk is being managed as claimed. For AI this means auditing models as well as processes, and it demands new skills from every line of defence.

In brief

  • Assurance comes from many providers (first-line self-assessment, second-line oversight and validation, internal audit, external auditors, certifiers, regulators). An assurance map shows where they overlap and where there are gaps.
  • Every key control is tested for design (would it work if operated as described?) and operating effectiveness (did it work, consistently, over the period?).
  • Re-performance is the strongest evidence, and for AI it means auditors recomputing metrics, querying models and running their own tests.
  • Sample sizes follow from simple probability: to be 95% confident that a control fails less than 10% of the time with zero exceptions found, test 29 instances.
  • External options are maturing: ISO/IEC 42001 certification, ISAE 3000 reports, independent bias audits, and EU AI Act conformity assessment. A certificate is evidence of a management system, not proof that a model is safe.
AuditBoardPractitioner

What assurance is

Assurance is an objective examination of evidence to provide an independent opinion on whether a subject matter (an AI governance framework, a specific model, a set of controls) meets criteria (a policy, a regulation, a standard such as ISO/IEC 42001). An engagement has three parties: the responsible party (management), the intended users (board, regulator, customers) and the assurance provider. It also has a stated level of confidence:

  • Reasonable assurance: a high but not absolute level of confidence, expressed positively (“in our opinion, the controls operated effectively”). It requires extensive testing.
  • Limited assurance: a lower level, expressed negatively (“nothing has come to our attention that causes us to believe…”). It relies mainly on inquiry and analytical procedures.

Internal audit opinions are usually graded (effective / needs improvement / ineffective, or similar). What matters is that each opinion rests on a documented evidence chain that another competent auditor could follow and reach the same conclusion.

The assurance map

No single function can assure AI alone, and duplicated testing is wasteful. An assurance map lays out each AI risk area against each assurance provider, showing coverage and its strength. It exposes three things: gaps (risk areas nobody tests), overlaps (the same control tested three times), and reliance opportunities (where internal audit can rely on second-line work after testing its quality). The IIA calls this combined assurance.

Internal audit's approach to AI

Under the IIA's Global Internal Audit Standards (effective January 2025), internal audit plans its work from a risk assessment of the organisation's audit universe. AI enters the universe in three ways, each needing a different kind of audit:

Entity-level

Framework audit

Is the AI governance framework designed and operating effectively across the firm? Policy, inventory, tiering, committee decision rights, second-line challenge, reporting to the board, AI literacy. Typically annual or biennial.

System-level

AI system audit

Deep-dive into a specific high-risk system, such as a credit decisioning model or a customer chatbot: data, model, controls, outcomes, human oversight, monitoring. Rotated across Tier 1 systems.

Cross-cutting

Thematic audit

One risk across many systems: “GenAI use across the bank”, “AI in third-party platforms”, “fairness monitoring in retail credit”, “AI incident management”. Good for spotting systemic issues.

Increasingly, internal audit also runs continuous auditing: automated analytics over inventory, validation schedules, monitoring outputs and override logs that flag anomalies between formal audits.

Testing controls: design, then operation

Each key control is tested twice over:

  • Test of design (TOD): if performed as described by competent people, would the control address the risk? Evaluated by walkthrough (following one instance end to end through the process and systems), inspection of the control description and supporting documents, and judgement about precision, frequency and thresholds. A control that fails its design test is not tested further: it is a finding.
  • Test of operating effectiveness (TOE): did the control operate as designed, consistently, throughout the period? Evaluated by testing a sample of instances. For automated controls, test the configuration once, then test the change-management and access controls that protect it.

How many to test? The mathematics of sampling

For a control that operates many times, auditors test a sample and infer about the population. For attribute sampling (each instance either operated correctly or it did not), with an acceptable (“tolerable”) deviation rate \(p_T\) and desired confidence \(C\), the sample size when no deviations are expected is the smallest \(n\) such that finding none would be unlikely if the true rate were \(p_T\):

$$ (1 - p_T)^n \le 1 - C \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = \left\lceil \frac{\ln(1-C)}{\ln(1-p_T)} \right\rceil. $$

At 95% confidence and a 10% tolerable rate, \(n = \ln 0.05 / \ln 0.9 = 28.4 \rightarrow\) 29. At 90% confidence, 22. At a 5% tolerable rate and 95% confidence, 59. If a deviation is found, the sample no longer supports the conclusion at that tolerance. You either extend the sample (using the binomial distribution to allow for one or more deviations) or conclude the control is not operating effectively. In practice, one deviation in a small sample of a key control is usually reported as a finding, with root-cause analysis to decide whether it is isolated or systemic.

Auditing the model, not just the process

AI assurance must go beyond checking that a validation report exists. The following techniques let an assurance team test the AI itself:

  • Metric re-performance: recompute discrimination, calibration, stability and fairness metrics from the source datasets and compare with those reported. Discrepancies point to data, code or reporting errors.
  • Paired testing (synthetic applicants): submit pairs of otherwise-identical test cases that differ only in a protected characteristic or a suspected proxy (name, postcode, gender-coded CV wording). Differences in outcome are direct evidence of disparate treatment. This is the algorithmic version of the “mystery shopping” audit studies long used in discrimination research.
  • Counterfactual stress testing: perturb inputs systematically (income ±10%, missing fields, extreme values) and check that behaviour is monotonic, stable and sensible.
  • Explanation audit: reconcile customer-facing reasons with model drivers (Chapter 05).
  • Boundary and population testing: review decisions just either side of the cut-off, and compare the production population with the development population (PSI).
  • Adversarial and red-team testing for GenAI, run or commissioned by assurance and independent of the build team's own testing.
  • Log analytics: full-population analysis of decision, override and guardrail logs, rather than samples.

Chapter 11

Trust: The Bank's Real Balance Sheet

The word credit comes from the Latin credere, “to believe”. A bank is a machine for turning belief into money: depositors believe they can have their money back on demand, and so the bank can lend it out for thirty years. Every other asset on the balance sheet depends on that one intangible. AI now sits in the middle of it.

In brief

  • Trust is not a soft asset for a bank. It is the precondition of the business model: maturity transformation works only while depositors believe it will.
  • Trust is asymmetric: it builds slowly through many good experiences and collapses quickly after one visible failure. In a digital, social-media world, the collapse can happen in hours (SVB lost about a quarter of its deposits in a single day in March 2023).
  • AI now mediates a large and growing share of the moments that build or destroy trust: credit decisions, fraud blocks, prices, service conversations. It works at scale, so one flaw becomes a million identical bad experiences.
  • Trust has three components (ability, benevolence, integrity), and each maps onto specific AI controls.
  • Following Onora O'Neill, the goal is not to demand trust but to be trustworthy and to give intelligent evidence of it. Responsible-AI governance, measurement and assurance are that evidence.
BoardPractitioner

Banking is the business of belief

Strip banking to its essentials and it is a promise. A bank takes short-term deposits that can be withdrawn at any moment and turns them into long-term loans that cannot be recalled. This maturity transformation is socially valuable: it lets households save safely and borrow to buy homes. But it rests on a structural fragility. No bank holds enough liquid assets to repay every depositor at once. The arrangement works because, on any given day, depositors believe it will work, and so they do not all ask for their money at once.

That is why the history of banking is a history of confidence and its collapse. Walter Bagehot's Lombard Street (1873), written after the failure of Overend, Gurney & Co. in 1866, set out the central-bank doctrine that still governs crisis management: lend freely, against good collateral, at a penalty rate. Its purpose is to restore belief. The City of London's old motto, dictum meum pactum, “my word is my bond”, describes the same foundation.

In September 2007 Northern Rock suffered the first run on a British bank in well over a century. Television pictures of queues outside branches did in days what years of funding-market strain had not. The bank was sound enough to be rescued. It was not trusted enough to survive. In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank showed what the digital era had changed: depositors tried to withdraw about $42 billion in a single day, roughly a quarter of the bank's deposits, coordinated partly through social media and executed through mobile apps. Around $100 billion more was queued for the next morning. The bank was closed before it opened. Trust, which once left through branch doors, now leaves at the speed of a notification.

Conduct scandals: trust lost slowly, then all at once

Runs are the acute form of trust failure. The chronic form is conduct. In the UK, mis-sold payment protection insurance cost the industry close to £40 billion in redress over more than a decade. The LIBOR and foreign-exchange rigging scandals brought multi-billion-dollar fines across global banks. At Wells Fargo, sales pressure led staff to open millions of accounts customers had not authorised, which brought a Federal Reserve asset cap in 2018 that constrained the bank's growth for seven years. None of these was a liquidity crisis. All of them lowered public trust in banks, raised the cost of regulation for the whole industry, and made each later failure more believable.

The pattern is instructive for AI. In each scandal a systematic process (a sales incentive, a submission practice, a product design) produced the same unfair outcome many times over, and it continued because nobody with power over it was measuring, or listening to, the harm. AI is a new and very efficient systematic process. A biased credit model, a manipulative retention algorithm or a chatbot that routinely misstates policy is a conduct scandal waiting for a journalist.

“Trust arrives on foot and leaves on horseback.”— Dutch proverb

Why trust is a balance-sheet asset

Accountants do not put trust on the balance sheet, but every major line depends on it:

  • Funding cost and stability. Retail deposits are the cheapest, most stable funding a bank has. Their stability, the “deposit franchise”, is a direct function of trust, and it is priced into liquidity regulation through outflow assumptions.
  • Customer lifetime value. Trusted banks retain customers, cross-sell and earn the benefit of the doubt when things go wrong.
  • Access to data. Open banking, open finance and every AI personalisation use case rely on customers agreeing to share data. People share data only with institutions they trust not to misuse it.
  • Regulatory latitude. Supervisors who trust a firm's governance grant model approvals, sandbox access and proportionate oversight. Those who do not impose skilled-person reviews, capital add-ons and business restrictions.
  • Talent. The engineers and data scientists who build AI increasingly choose employers on values.
  • Valuation. Markets discount institutions whose conduct risk they cannot size.

What trust is made of

The most widely used model of organisational trust, from Mayer, Davis and Schoorman (1995), says that we trust a party to the extent that we perceive three things:

Competence

Ability

“Can they do what they promise?” Skill, reliability and accuracy within the relevant domain.

AI expression: models that work: accurate, robust, validated, monitored; services that do not fail; fraud caught, genuine payments not blocked.

Care

Benevolence

“Do they have my interests at heart, beyond profit?”

AI expression: fair outcomes; no exploiting of vulnerability or inertia; AI that helps customers reach their goals, which is the Consumer Duty in practice; human help when needed.

Character

Integrity

“Do they follow principles I find acceptable, consistently, and tell me the truth?”

AI expression: honest disclosure of AI use; explanations that match reality; commitments the chatbot makes are honoured; red lines that hold under pressure.

David Maister's “trust equation” adds a useful sceptical term:

$$ \text{Trustworthiness} = \frac{\text{Credibility} + \text{Reliability} + \text{Intimacy}}{\text{Self-orientation}}. $$

The denominator matters most. However competent and reliable an institution is, trust falls sharply if customers believe it is optimising for itself at their expense. That is exactly the suspicion that opaque AI invites. A customer declined by an algorithm, offered a price by an algorithm, or nudged by an algorithm cannot see whose interests the objective function serves. Without evidence to the contrary, many will assume the worst.

How AI plays into trust

AI changes the trust equation in banking in five ways.

1. It sits at the moments of truth

The interactions that most shape a customer's view of their bank are the moments when something is at stake: a mortgage decision, a blocked card abroad, a disputed transaction, a hardship conversation. These are exactly the moments being automated. Each AI decision is a trust event.

2. It turns individual errors into correlated failures

A human underwriter's mistake affects one customer. A model's systematic error affects every customer with the same profile, identically and at once. Trust failures stop being random and become correlated, which is exactly the kind of risk that turns incidents into scandals and scandals into regulatory actions. AI governance is, among other things, the control of correlated conduct risk.

3. It makes the bank harder to understand

Trust in a counterpart you cannot inspect depends on signals of trustworthiness. Opaque AI removes the natural signal (a person explaining their reasoning), so the institution must replace it with others: explanations, disclosures, contestability, and published evidence of governance and outcomes.

4. It attacks trust from outside

Generative AI arms fraudsters. Voice clones defeat voice authentication. Deepfake video conferences have been used to trick employees into multi-million-dollar transfers, as in the case of the engineering firm Arup in Hong Kong in 2024. Synthetic identities pass onboarding checks. Every successful attack of this kind erodes customers' confidence that their bank can protect them, so AI-enabled fraud defence is itself a trust investment. The FCA's Mills Review names amplified fraud and cyber risk as one of the four main AI shifts to 2030.

5. It can build trust faster than any previous technology

The same capabilities, used well, strengthen every component of trust: faster and more accurate fraud detection (ability); early identification of customers heading for financial difficulty, with proactive support (benevolence); consistent decisions free of individual prejudice (integrity); round-the-clock service; and inclusion of “thin-file” customers through responsibly used alternative data. The point of responsible AI is not to slow this down. It is to make sure the trust-building use cases win.

From trust to trustworthiness: Onora O'Neill's challenge

In her 2002 Reith Lectures, the philosopher Onora O'Neill argued that the goal of institutions should not be to “build trust”, which cannot be commanded, but to be trustworthy and to make that trustworthiness assessable. Trust, she argued, should be placed intelligently, in response to evidence. She criticised audit cultures that produce paperwork rather than insight, and called instead for intelligent accountability: forms of accountability that give real information about whether an institution deserves trust.

That is the deeper rationale for everything in this guide. An AI inventory, a fairness test, a validation report, an assurance opinion, a transparency report and a board dashboard are not compliance artefacts. They are evidence of trustworthiness, given to the people who need it: customers, supervisors, investors, employees and the board. A bank that can show, specifically and with evidence, how it makes sure its AI treats people fairly and works as intended has something to offer when an incident happens. A bank that can only point to a page of principles does not.

The trust dividend of governance

Governance is usually costed as friction. The trust lens shows the return. Ex ante, it lowers the likelihood and reach of trust-destroying incidents. Ex post, it changes how an incident is interpreted. Recall Chapter 03's distinction between criticism of competence and criticism of values: when a firm can show it tested, monitored, escalated and fixed, an incident is read as a competence lapse that has been dealt with, not as evidence of bad values. The first is survivable. The second is often not.

AI Risk Appetite: Setting, Measuring, Cascading

Risk appetite is how a board says, in advance and in writing, how much risk it will accept in pursuit of the firm's objectives. For AI that is unusually hard. The risk cuts across every category, much of it resists quantification, and the danger of taking too little risk (falling behind) is as real as the danger of taking too much.

In brief

  • Distinguish capacity (the most risk the firm could bear), appetite (what it chooses to seek), tolerance (the acceptable variation around appetite, the amber zone) and limits (the operational cascade).
  • AI risk appetite needs both qualitative statements and quantitative metrics, and it must cascade from the board to individual systems.
  • Use “zero appetite” only for things that can be controlled as binary: prohibited uses, yes; model error, no. False zero-tolerance produces under-reporting, not safety.
  • Some of the most useful AI metrics are two-sided: an override rate that is too low is as worrying as one that is too high.
  • Set thresholds from the statistics of the metric (control limits), not by instinct, so that red means something and noise does not trigger escalation.
  • Include an appetite for innovation. A statement that only restricts will be ignored by the business or will quietly cede ground to competitors.

Why AI risk appetite is hard: twelve nuances

Most firms' first attempt at an AI risk appetite statement is either a paragraph of good intentions that constrains nothing, or a list of prohibitions that ignores the opportunity. The nuances below separate a useful statement from a decorative one.

1. AI is not a risk type; it is a risk amplifier

AI risk shows up as model risk, conduct risk, operational risk, compliance risk, data and privacy risk, third-party risk and reputational risk. A standalone “AI risk appetite” that ignores the existing appetites creates contradictions. The better design is an AI lens across existing appetites: the model risk appetite (SS1/23 expects the board to set one) covers AI models, the conduct appetite covers AI-driven customer outcomes, the operational resilience tolerances cover AI in important business services, and a short AI-specific statement covers what is genuinely new (prohibited uses, GenAI, agents, ethical red lines).

2. Qualitative statements must be convertible into metrics

“We have a low appetite for AI bias” is meaningless until someone can say what number would breach it. Every qualitative statement should come with at least one metric, a threshold and an owner, even if the first-generation metric is imperfect. Where no metric is possible, specify the process that gives assurance (for example “all Tier 1 systems independently validated before use”) and measure compliance with the process.

3. Zero appetite is for binary things

Zero appetite is right for conduct that can be fully controlled and is clearly wrong: EU AI Act prohibited practices, use of customer data in unapproved public GenAI tools, deployment without required approval. It is wrong for outcomes that are statistically inevitable: a model will make errors, a chatbot will sometimes misunderstand. Declaring “zero tolerance for hallucination” does not stop hallucinations. It stops people reporting them. Use low appetite with explicit numerical tolerance instead.

4. Two-sided metrics

Many AI metrics have a healthy band, not a direction. Human override rates: too low suggests rubber-stamping (automation bias), too high suggests a model that is not trusted or not fit for purpose. Chatbot refusal rates: too low risks harmful or out-of-scope answers, too high means the service is not serving. Fraud model alert rates: too low misses fraud, too high blocks genuine customers. Appetite statements and dashboards need to show bands, not single thresholds.

5. The appetite for innovation

A bank that does not adopt AI still has AI risk. It falls behind on fraud detection, cost and customer experience while its customers and competitors, and its fraudsters, adopt it anyway. The FCA's Mills Review describes a market being reshaped by agentic consumer journeys. A credible statement includes a positive appetite: for example “we will pursue AI to improve customer outcomes and efficiency, with moderate appetite for internal productivity uses and a structured, gated path to customer-facing use”. This also gives the risk function a mandate to enable as well as constrain.

6. Leading and lagging indicators

Lagging indicators (incidents, complaints, losses, regulatory findings) confirm harm after it has happened. Leading indicators (overdue validations, unmonitored systems, inventory gaps, fairness drift, training completion) warn before it does. A board dashboard dominated by lagging indicators will always be green until it is suddenly red. Aim for at least half the AI KRIs to be leading.

7. Tiering changes the threshold

A PSI of 0.2 on a Tier 4 internal forecasting tool and on a Tier 1 credit model are not the same event. Thresholds and escalation routes should scale with tier: the same metric, different appetite.

8. Aggregation and concentration

Thirty systems, each individually within appetite, can together be out of appetite: for example if all of them depend on the same foundation-model provider, use the same bureau data feed or share a flawed feature. The source modules make the point for models generally: aggregate model risk depends on interdependencies and shared assumptions. Include portfolio-level metrics (provider concentration, share of customer decisions made by unvalidated models) as well as system-level ones.

9. Statistical noise versus signal

Fairness ratios on small segments and monthly hallucination rates on samples move randomly. A fixed threshold on a noisy metric produces false alarms that teach people to ignore the dashboard. Use confidence intervals, persistence rules (two consecutive breaches) and control limits derived from the metric's own variability (below).

10. Time and duration

How long is a breach tolerable? A day's spike in chatbot errors after a product change, fixed within hours, is different from a fairness gap that persists for a year. Appetite statements should say how long remediation may take by severity, and the board should see ageing.

11. Unknown unknowns and emerging risk

Appetite frameworks are calibrated on known risks. Generative and agentic AI keep producing new failure modes. Complement metrics with a standing emerging-risk review and with scenario analysis that asks “what would have to be true for this to go badly wrong?”

12. Appetite must have consequences

An appetite breach must trigger something: management action within a set time, escalation, a restriction on new deployments, a pause. If nothing ever happens when a metric goes red, the appetite statement is decoration. The escalation matrix is part of the appetite, not an appendix to it.

Setting thresholds with statistics: control charts

Many AI KRIs are proportions measured on samples: the hallucination rate in a weekly QA sample, the approval rate for a group, the override rate for a team. Statistical process control provides a principled threshold. For a proportion with long-run average \(\bar p\) measured on samples of size \(n\), the p-chart control limits are

$$ \text{UCL} = \bar p + 3\sqrt{\frac{\bar p(1-\bar p)}{n}}, \qquad \text{LCL} = \max\!\Big(0,\; \bar p - 3\sqrt{\frac{\bar p(1-\bar p)}{n}}\Big). $$

A point outside the ±3σ limits has roughly a 0.3% chance of occurring by chance if the process is stable, so it is a genuine signal. Western Electric-style run rules (for example, eight consecutive points on one side of the centre line) catch sustained drift before any single point breaches. This gives three benefits: thresholds defensible to auditors and regulators, fewer false alarms, and earlier detection of slow deterioration. Appetite thresholds (what the board will accept) and control limits (what is statistically unusual) are different things, and the dashboard should show both.

KRIs cover the everyday. Appetite must also cover severe but plausible events, which KRIs rarely show until too late. Scenario analysis is the standard operational-risk tool: a structured workshop estimates how often an event might occur and how much it would cost, drawing on internal incidents, external cases (Chapter 14) and expert judgement. The scenario estimates can be combined into an aggregate annual loss distribution with the classic loss distribution approach. The number of events \(N\) in a year is Poisson with rate \(\lambda\), and each severity \(X_i\) is lognormal:

$$ L = \sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i, \qquad N \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \qquad \ln X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2). $$

Monte Carlo simulation gives the expected annual loss and tail percentiles (for example the 99th or 99.9th), which can be compared directly with risk capacity and with the quantitative part of the appetite statement. The numbers are uncertain, but the exercise disciplines judgement, exposes which scenarios drive the tail, and gives the board an order of magnitude to set appetite against.

The appetite-setting process

  1. Top-down: the board sets strategic intent (where AI should create value) and risk capacity (what the firm cannot afford).
  2. Bottom-up: the risk function assesses the current profile (inventory, KRIs, findings, scenarios) and historical variability.
  3. Proposal: the second line drafts statements and thresholds, reconciled with existing model, conduct and operational appetites.
  4. Challenge: business lines test for practicality, internal audit for measurability and data quality, and the executive AI committee debates the trade-offs.
  5. Approval: the board risk committee recommends and the board approves.
  6. Cascade: limits and thresholds are embedded in business plans, system approvals and monitoring tooling.
  7. Monitor and report: monthly to the executive and quarterly to the board (Chapter 13).
  8. Review: at least annually, and on trigger events such as a major incident, a regulatory change (for example the July 2026 Omnibus), a new AI capability or a strategic shift.
Assurance angle

Auditing the risk appetite framework for AI

  • Does every qualitative AI appetite statement have at least one measurable metric with a threshold and an owner?
  • Are thresholds justified (statistical, regulatory, peer or loss-based) and documented, or arbitrary?
  • Is the KRI data accurate and complete? Re-perform two KRIs from source (BCBS 239 principles apply).
  • When KRIs breached, did the prescribed escalation and action occur within the set time?
  • Is the AI appetite consistent with the model risk, conduct and operational resilience appetites?
  • Has the board reviewed and re-approved the appetite in the last 12 months, and after any trigger events?
Chapter 13

Reporting to the Board

A board cannot oversee what it cannot see. Most AI reporting fails in one of two ways: too technical to support a decision, or so aggregated that everything is green until something goes publicly wrong. Good board reporting is decision-focused, honest about uncertainty, and ties every metric back to the appetite the board itself approved.

In brief

  • The board's job is oversight: approve the AI policy and risk appetite, make sure competent people and adequate resources are in place, challenge management, and hold named executives to account.
  • A quarterly AI risk report should answer four questions: Are we within appetite? What changed? What could go wrong next? What do you need us to decide?
  • Beware “watermelon” reporting, green on the outside and red inside. Ask for the underlying distribution, the trend and the assurance status behind every green.
  • For UK regulated firms, board and committee papers, minutes and follow-ups are the core evidence of senior managers' reasonable steps under SM&CR.
  • Directors need enough AI literacy to challenge. That is now a governance expectation, not a nice-to-have.
BoardRisk & company secretariat

What the board is responsible for

Across the regulatory frameworks, the board's AI responsibilities converge:

  • Set direction: approve the AI strategy and ensure it fits the firm's purpose, values and risk appetite.
  • Set boundaries: approve the AI policy, prohibited uses and risk appetite (Chapter 12). The PRA's SS1/23 expects the board to set model risk appetite and to have an accountable senior manager for the model risk framework.
  • Resource and competence: ensure the first, second and third lines have the skills, tools and authority to manage and assure AI. The source modules note that the board and senior management must ensure qualified resources are available across the model lifecycle.
  • Oversee: receive regular, reliable information on the AI risk profile against appetite, and challenge it.
  • Hold accountable: through SM&CR in the UK, and equivalent accountability regimes elsewhere (Ireland's Individual Accountability Framework, Australia's FAR, Hong Kong's Manager-in-Charge regime).
  • Conduct outcomes: under the Consumer Duty, the board approves an annual assessment of whether the firm is delivering good outcomes, which must now cover AI-driven decisions where material.

Principles for AI risk reporting

The Basel Committee's BCBS 239 principles for risk data aggregation and reporting were written for credit and market risk, but they apply directly to AI. Board reports should be:

Accurate

Reconciled to source data; metrics defined consistently; subject to validation. A board that later learns its AI dashboard was wrong stops trusting the risk function.

Comprehensive

Covers the whole inventory, including vendor and embedded AI, and all material risk types. Says explicitly what it does not cover.

Clear & useful

Decision-focused. A narrative explains what the numbers mean. Technical detail goes to appendices.

Timely & frequent

Quarterly as standard; ad hoc for critical incidents; monthly to executive committees.

Forward-looking

Trends, emerging risks, regulatory horizon, scenario results, not only last quarter's snapshot.

Honest about uncertainty

Confidence intervals where metrics are sampled; assurance status of each number; known data gaps.

The quarterly AI risk report: a structure

  1. Executive summary (one page): overall status against appetite, the three things the board most needs to know, decisions requested.
  2. Appetite dashboard: every AI KRI with RAG status, trend, and the board-approved threshold.
  3. Portfolio profile: inventory by tier, type, business line and build/buy/embed; new approvals and retirements; systems in use under conditions.
  4. Top risks and emerging risks: the five most significant AI risks, with owner, trajectory and mitigation; the horizon scan.
  5. Incidents and near misses: material events, root causes, customer impact, redress, lessons.
  6. Assurance and findings: validation coverage, audit opinions, open findings by severity and age, overdue actions named by owner.
  7. Regulatory developments: changes and their impact (for example the Omnibus deferral, FCA reasonable-steps guidance), and readiness against deadlines.
  8. Decisions and approvals: Tier 1 approvals for noting or decision, exceptions to policy, appetite changes.

Good and poor reporting

The same facts can be reported in ways that enable oversight or prevent it:

Poor

“AI risk remains within appetite. 97% of models are validated. No material AI incidents occurred in the quarter. Work continues on the GenAI framework.”

All green. But 97% of what? The 3% could be the three most important customer models. “No material incidents” depends on a definition the board has never seen. “Work continues” tells the board nothing about risk.

Good

“Two KRIs are amber. Validation coverage of Tier 1–2 systems is 96% (target 100%); the gap is 3 systems, including the mortgage affordability model, 47 days overdue due to validator capacity. We request approval to fund two external validators.”

It names the gap and its significance, explains the cause, and asks for a decision within the board's power. The board can act.

Spotting a watermelon

“Watermelon” reporting, green outside and red inside, comes from aggregation, lenient definitions or self-assessment without assurance. Directors can test for it:

  • Ask for the distribution, not the average. “All models within fairness tolerance on average” can hide one far outside.
  • Ask what would turn it red, and whether that has ever happened. A metric that has never been red may be badly calibrated.
  • Ask who assured the number. A self-assessed green is weaker than one re-performed by internal audit.
  • Compare with other signals. Green AI KRIs alongside rising AI-related complaints is a contradiction to explore.
  • Visit the front line. Directors who watch a credit referral or a chatbot QA session learn things no dashboard shows.

Reasonable steps: building the evidence file

Under the UK's SM&CR, a senior manager can be held accountable if a breach occurs in their area and they did not take the steps a senior manager in their position could reasonably be expected to take. The FCA has not yet issued AI-specific guidance on what reasonable steps are. The Treasury Select Committee has asked for it by the end of 2026. Existing enforcement principles already point to what a senior manager with AI in their area should be able to show:

  • Clarity of responsibility: their statement of responsibilities and the responsibilities map name the AI systems and processes in scope.
  • Knowledge: they have received training and regular, adequate MI about those systems, and can show they read and understood it.
  • Challenge: minutes show questions asked and follow-ups demanded, not just papers noted.
  • Action: when MI showed problems, they acted in proportion and on time: escalated, resourced, restricted or paused.
  • Delegation with oversight: where they delegated, they satisfied themselves the delegate was competent and monitored the outcome.
  • Frameworks: appropriate policies, controls and assurance exist and are tested.

Minutes are evidence

“The committee noted the AI risk report” is evidence that a paper was circulated. “The committee challenged the amber status of validation coverage, asked the CRO to report back within four weeks on the mortgage affordability model, and approved funding for two external validators” is evidence of reasonable steps. Company secretaries and committee chairs should minute AI discussions with that standard in mind.

What must be escalated immediately

Quarterly reporting is not enough for some events. The escalation policy should require immediate notification to the relevant SMF, the committee chair and, where appropriate, the board, for:

  • Any prohibited-use breach or deployment without required approval.
  • A critical AI incident: material customer detriment, a likely regulatory breach, or significant media exposure.
  • Any event requiring regulatory notification (a GDPR breach within 72 hours, an EU AI Act serious incident, an operational-resilience incident).
  • Activation of a kill switch on a Tier 1 system.
  • A red-line challenge: a customer, partner or authority demanding a use the firm has ruled out.

Board competence

Boards are increasingly expected to show that they collectively have enough understanding of technology risk to oversee it. For AI that means a training programme (fundamentals; risks and failure modes; the regulatory landscape; how the firm's own framework works; what good reporting looks like), a skills matrix that shows AI and data competence, access to independent expertise when needed, and experiential learning such as demonstrations, site visits and table-top incident exercises. The source modules note that it is still rare to see AI ethicists on boards. Some firms address this with advisory panels reporting to a board committee.

Twenty questions every board should ask about AI

Strategy & appetite

  1. Where is AI creating value for us and for our customers, and where do we intend it to?
  2. What is our AI risk appetite, and which metrics would tell us we had breached it?
  3. What have we decided never to do with AI, and who can change that?
  4. What is the risk of not adopting AI in fraud, service and cost?
  5. Which competitors or new entrants are using AI to change customer expectations?

Inventory & accountability

  1. How many AI systems do we have, and how confident are we that the number is complete?
  2. Which are Tier 1, and who is personally accountable for each?
  3. How much of our AI comes from vendors, and what rights do we have over it?
  4. Which important business services depend on a single AI provider?
  5. Which of our systems fall within the EU AI Act's high-risk categories, and are we on track for December 2027?

Outcomes & fairness

  1. How do we know our AI treats different customer groups fairly? Which fairness measure did we choose, and why?
  2. Can we explain to a declined customer why they were declined, and what they can do?
  3. How are vulnerable customers identified and protected in AI-mediated journeys?
  4. What do customers complain about when AI is involved?
  5. Is human oversight real? What are our override rates?

Control & assurance

  1. What was our last AI incident, and what did we learn?
  2. Can we switch off any customer-facing AI system within an hour? Have we tested it?
  3. When did internal audit last re-perform tests on our most important model?
  4. Do our second and third lines have the skills to challenge our data scientists?
  5. What evidence of our AI's trustworthiness could we publish tomorrow?

External reporting as trust evidence

A growing number of large companies publish annual responsible-AI or transparency reports. Microsoft has done so since 2024. Banks increasingly include AI governance in annual reports, sustainability disclosures and Consumer Duty communications. External reporting is a commitment device: once published, principles and metrics are harder to quietly abandon. In O'Neill's terms, it is also how an institution offers intelligent evidence of trustworthiness to people who cannot inspect it from the inside. The discipline this guide describes (inventory, tiering, testing, monitoring, assurance, appetite, reporting) is what makes such disclosures credible rather than decorative.

Chapter 14

Case Files

Every principle in this guide was learned the hard way by someone. These cases are grouped by the lesson they teach, and each is tied to the control that would most directly have prevented or contained it. Where matters are ongoing, the allegations are not findings.

In brief: seven patterns across the cases

  1. You own your vendors' AI. Workday's customers, Air Canada, Rite Aid. Contract for testing data, audit rights and change notification.
  2. Historical data reproduces historical bias. St George's, Amazon, Workday allegations. Test outcomes, not just inputs.
  3. Explainability is a customer and regulatory requirement. Apple Card: lawful but opaque still damaged trust.
  4. Human review must be genuine. Deloitte Australia, Uber Tempe: outputs reached the world without adequate verification.
  5. Principles need decision rights and external checks. Google's principles revision; ethics-team departures.
  6. Deployment context is part of the risk. Tay, consumer chatbots, facial recognition in retail.
  7. Red lines will be tested. Google 2025; Anthropic 2026. Decide in advance who can move them, and how.
UK · 1988

St George's Hospital Medical School

A program used to screen medical-school applicants had been built to reproduce past admissions decisions. The Commission for Racial Equality found it discriminated against women and applicants with non-European names.

Lesson: Training on past human decisions automates past human bias. This was known decades before deep learning.

Controls: DAT-02, DEV-03

Reported 2018

Amazon recruiting tool

A CV-screening model trained on a decade of male-dominated hiring learned to penalise signals linked to women, such as “women's” in a CV. Engineers could not reliably remove the effect, and the tool was abandoned.

Lesson: Removing a protected attribute does not remove its proxies.

Controls: DAT-03, DEV-03

2023 → ongoing

Mobley v. Workday

An applicant alleges that Workday's AI screening discriminated on age, race and disability. A nationwide collective of applicants aged 40+ was preliminarily certified in May 2025. On 22 June 2026 the court largely denied Workday's latest motion to dismiss and ordered it to identify customers who enabled HiredScore AI features. There has been no finding of liability.

Lesson: A vendor may be treated as the employer's agent, and customers can be drawn in.

Controls: TPR-01, TPR-02, MON-02

2019–2021

Apple Card / Goldman Sachs

Public complaints that women received lower credit limits than their husbands led to a New York DFS investigation. It found no unlawful discrimination but criticised poor transparency and customer service when people asked why.

Lesson: A lawful model still creates regulatory and reputational risk if you cannot explain its outcomes.

Controls: DEV-05, DEP-05

2024

Moffatt v. Air Canada

The airline's website chatbot wrongly told a bereaved customer he could claim a bereavement fare retrospectively. Air Canada argued the chatbot was responsible for its own statements. The British Columbia tribunal rejected that and found negligent misrepresentation.

Lesson: You own everything your customer-facing AI says.

Controls: DEV-06, DEP-03, MON-03

2025

Deloitte Australia

A A$440,000 report for the Department of Employment and Workplace Relations contained fabricated references and a made-up quotation from a Federal Court judgment. A revised version disclosed Azure OpenAI use, and Deloitte refunded the final instalment (reported at about A$97,000).

Lesson: Hallucination is now a professional-quality and contractual issue, particularly for firms that advise others.

Controls: GOV-01, DAT-04, MON-03

2021

Zillow Offers

An algorithmic home-buying business relied on price forecasts that could not keep pace with a turning market. It was wound down with large write-downs and job losses.

Lesson: Model risk is business risk. Outside its training conditions a model can be existential for a business line.

Controls: MON-01, DEV-04, VAL-04

FTC · 2023

Rite Aid facial recognition

In-store facial recognition generated many false matches, disproportionately affecting women and people of colour, and the company took no reasonable steps to prevent harm. It was banned from using facial recognition for security for five years.

Lesson: Deployers, not only developers, must test accuracy and bias before use.

Controls: TPR-01, DEV-03, DEV-01

2020 →

Clearview AI

Clearview scraped billions of online images to build a facial recognition database sold mainly to law enforcement. Several European data protection authorities imposed fines and orders; the UK ICO's enforcement went through lengthy appeals.

Lesson: Publicly available data is not lawfully usable data.

Controls: DAT-01

2020 · 2022

Facial recognition moratoria

After the murder of George Floyd, IBM exited general-purpose facial recognition, and Amazon and Microsoft paused police sales. Microsoft later restricted facial recognition access and retired emotion inference under its Responsible AI Standard v2.

Lesson: Responsible AI can mean choosing not to sell a capability at all.

Controls: GOV-01, INV-04

2016

Microsoft Tay

A Twitter chatbot that learned from interactions was manipulated into posting racist and offensive content within a day of launch and was withdrawn. It is widely credited with shaping Microsoft's later responsible AI programme.

Lesson: A system that learns from the public must be red-teamed against coordinated abuse.

Controls: DEV-06, DEP-04

2024 → ongoing

Consumer chatbots & vulnerable users

US lawsuits against companion and general chatbot providers allege that their products contributed to harm to teenagers and other vulnerable users. They are largely unresolved, but have already driven age assurance, parental controls and changes to how models respond to people in distress.

Lesson: Foreseeable misuse and vulnerable users belong in the risk assessment.

Controls: DEV-01, DEV-06, MON-03

2018 → 2025

Google: Project Maven and the AI Principles

Employee protests over Pentagon drone-imagery work led Google to publish AI Principles in 2018, with a list of applications it would not pursue. In February 2025 that list was removed, citing global competition and democracies leading in AI. Human rights groups and US senators objected.

Lesson: Principles without external enforcement can be rewritten. Watch what changes, not just what is published.

Controls: GOV-01, GOV-06

2026 → ongoing

Anthropic and the US Department of Defense

Renegotiation broke down when Anthropic declined to drop usage restrictions on mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons. Agencies were directed to stop using its products, and the DoD designated it a supply-chain risk. On 27 August 2026 a federal judge ruled the designation unlawful (First Amendment retaliation; lack of due process); related litigation continues. OpenAI's replacement deal drew criticism, and both CEOs expressed some regret.

Lesson: Supporters see red lines held at real cost; critics argue a vendor should not constrain an elected government. For buyers, it also shows provider concentration risk.

Controls: GOV-06, TPR-04

2020–2023

Ethics teams under pressure

The departures of Timnit Gebru and Margaret Mitchell from Google's ethical AI team, and later reorganisations of dedicated ethics teams at Microsoft and Meta, fuelled debate over whether central ethics teams have real influence. The companies said responsibilities were being embedded into product teams.

Lesson: The independence, authority and resourcing of the responsible-AI function matter as much as its existence.

Controls: GOV-03, GOV-05

2016

COMPAS and “Machine Bias”

ProPublica reported that a US recidivism score had much higher false-positive rates for Black defendants. The vendor showed the score was similarly calibrated across groups. Both were right, which the impossibility theorem explains.

Lesson: Choose, justify and disclose your fairness criterion. You cannot satisfy them all.

Controls: DEV-03, DEV-01

2019

Obermeyer et al.: health-risk algorithm

A widely used US algorithm predicted future healthcare cost as a proxy for health need. Because less had historically been spent on Black patients with equal illness, it under-referred them to extra care.

Lesson: The target variable is the most consequential design choice. Accurate prediction of the wrong thing is harmful.

Controls: DEV-01, VAL-01

2018

Gender Shades

Buolamwini and Gebru found commercial facial-analysis systems had error rates as high as roughly 35% for darker-skinned women against under 1% for lighter-skinned men. Vendors improved after publication.

Lesson: Aggregate accuracy hides subgroup failure. Test intersectionally.

Controls: DEV-03, DAT-02

Netherlands · 2013–2021

Childcare benefits scandal

A risk-scoring system and aggressive enforcement wrongly flagged thousands of families as fraudsters, disproportionately those with dual nationality, demanding repayments with devastating consequences. The government resigned in January 2021.

Lesson: Algorithmic bias plus absent contestability can become a crisis of state legitimacy.

Controls: DEV-03, DEP-05, MON-02

England · 2020

Exam-grading algorithm

With exams cancelled, an algorithm moderated teacher-assessed grades using schools' historical results, systematically downgrading students from historically lower-performing schools. After protests, it was withdrawn in favour of teacher assessments.

Lesson: Individual fairness matters. People will not accept being judged by the past performance of others like them.

Controls: DEV-01, DEP-05

Australia · 2016–2023

Robodebt

An automated scheme raised welfare debts by averaging annual income data across fortnights, reversing the burden of proof. It was found unlawful. A Royal Commission (2023) condemned the scheme, and settlements and refunds ran to well over A$1 billion.

Lesson: Automation can scale an unlawful premise. Legal validation of the decision logic is a control.

Controls: DEV-01, VAL-01, INV-04

2012

JPMorgan “London Whale”

Trading losses of more than $6 billion in the Chief Investment Office were compounded by a new VaR model built with manual spreadsheet steps and a formula error that roughly halved measured risk.

Lesson: Implementation error in a model is invisible without independent re-performance and controlled implementation.

Controls: VAL-01, DEV-07, VAL-03

2012

Knight Capital

A deployment error reactivated obsolete trading code; about $440 million was lost in 45 minutes and the firm had to be rescued.

Lesson: Change management and kill switches are model controls. The analogue for agentic AI is direct.

Controls: VAL-03, DEP-04, DEP-06

2018

Cambridge Analytica

Data harvested from Facebook users via a personality quiz was used for psychologically targeted political advertising. Whatever the targeting's effect, the scandal did lasting damage to Facebook's brand and public trust.

Lesson: Privacy failures and manipulation risk compound, and reputational damage follows the data.

Controls: DAT-01, TPR-02

2014

Facebook emotional contagion experiment

Researchers altered users' feeds to show more positive or negative posts, without consent, and measured mood contagion. The outcry centred on consent and manipulation.

Lesson: Criticism of values (“they knew”) is harder to recover from than criticism of competence.

Controls: DEV-01, GOV-03

2018

Uber self-driving fatality, Tempe

A test vehicle failed to classify a pedestrian in time, emergency braking had been disabled, and the safety driver was distracted. The pedestrian was killed.

Lesson: Automation bias is lethal in critical systems. Design for vigilance; do not assume it.

Controls: DEP-01, DEV-04

Hong Kong · 2024

Deepfake video-conference fraud (Arup)

An employee was tricked into transferring about US$25 million after a video call in which the “CFO” and colleagues were deepfakes.

Lesson: Generative AI attacks authentication and trust itself. Payment controls must assume seeing is no longer believing.

Controls: bank payment controls; out-of-band verification

What structured good practice looks like

Alongside the failures are examples of organisations making deliberate responsible-AI choices. They are largely self-reported. The most credible programmes combine published standards with measurable evidence: transparency reports, independent audits or certifications, and documented cases where a product was modified, delayed or not launched.

Microsoft

Responsible AI Standard v2 (2022); Office of Responsible AI; sensitive-uses review process; annual Responsible AI Transparency Report.

Salesforce

Office of Ethical and Humane Use; published acceptable-use policies for its AI products.

IBM

AI Ethics Board with cross-business decision rights; published principles on trust and transparency.

Frontier labs

Capability thresholds that trigger stronger safeguards; pre-deployment testing by bodies such as the UK AI Security Institute.

ISO/IEC 42001 certification

Increasingly obtained by major cloud and AI providers as third-party evidence of AI management systems.

The test for all of them

Can the organisation point to a product it changed, delayed or did not launch because of its principles? That is the evidence that separates governance from marketing.
