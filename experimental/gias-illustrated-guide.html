    Prologue

    How the Standards are built

    Published by The Institute of Internal Auditors on 9 January 2024, the Global Internal Audit Standards are the core of the International Professional Practices Framework (IPPF).

    A framework, the IIA says, is a structural blueprint: a coherent system for developing, interpreting and applying a body of knowledge. The IPPF has three layers. The Global Internal Audit Standards sit at its heart. Topical Requirements are mandatory when an engagement's scope covers one of the identified topics. Global Guidance (Practice Guides and GTAGs) is supplemental and non-mandatory.

    The Standards apply to anyone who provides internal audit services, whether they are employees, outsourced providers or a mix of both. The chief audit executive (CAE) is accountable for the function's conformance. Every internal auditor is responsible for the principles that touch their own work, mainly in Domains II and V.

    “At the heart of the Standards are 15 guiding principles that enable effective internal auditing.GIAS · About the IPPF · p. 5

    The anatomy of a standard

    Domains II–V share one structure. Each Principle is a broad description of a group of requirements. Under it sit Standards, and each standard has three parts: Requirements, which are mandatory (MUST); Considerations for Implementation, which describe common and preferred practice (SHOULD and MAY); and Examples of Evidence of Conformance, which help a function prepare for quality assessments. Domain III adds a fourth part, Essential Conditions: things the board and senior management must do for the function to succeed.

    Who carries the weight?

    One square for each “must” sentence in a standard's Requirements, coloured by who it addresses (the chief audit executive or internal auditors). Domain III also adds the board's and senior management's essential-condition bullets. Domain II and Domain V fall mostly on individual auditors, while Domains III and IV fall on the CAE and the board. The classification is automatic and approximate, based on the grammatical subject of each sentence.

    The public interest

    The Standards set internal auditing in a wider frame than the organisation that pays for it. Internal audit's main job is to strengthen governance, risk management and control, but its effects reach further, into public trust in organisations and in the systems around them. The Standards themselves are set in the public interest, through due process overseen by the independent IPPF Oversight Council.

    I
    Domain I

    The Purpose of Internal Auditing

    One paragraph that sums up the profession. The Standards call it a tool for understanding and articulating the value of internal auditing.

    Plate 4 · Generative

    The lantern of assurance

    Internal auditing “strengthens the organization’s ability to create, protect, and sustain value” (GIAS, Domain I, p. 15). Four beams leave the lantern: assurance, advice, insight and foresight. Turn a beam off and watch the organisation dim. Move your cursor to steer the light.
    Internal auditing enhances…
    1. Successful achievement of objectives
    2. Governance, risk management and control processes
    3. Decision-making and oversight
    4. Reputation and credibility with stakeholders
    5. Ability to serve the public interest
    …and is most effective when
    1. It is performed by competent professionals conforming with Standards set in the public interest
    2. The function is independently positioned with direct accountability to the board
    3. Auditors are free from undue influence and committed to objective assessments

    Each condition maps onto a later domain: II (competence and ethics), III (positioning) and IV–V (doing the work well).

    ✦
    Remember this

    Domain I in four lines

    1. Internal audit exists to help the organisation create, protect and sustain value.
    2. It does so through assurance, advice, insight and foresight for the board and management.
    3. Its reach extends beyond the organisation, to the public interest.
    4. It works best when it is competent, independent and free from undue influence.

    Remember this

    Domain II in five lines

    1. Integrity is the foundation: tell the truth, disclose all material facts, and show courage under pressure.
    2. Objectivity is personal. Know your biases, refuse impairing gifts, and observe the 12-month cooling-off period before assuring work you once owned.
    3. Competency is never finished. Only take on work you can do, and keep up your CPD, including ethics.
    4. Due care means prudent and skeptical, not infallible. Seek more evidence when something doesn’t add up.
    5. Confidentiality: unrestricted access does not mean unrestricted use.

          ✦
          Remember this

          Domain III in five lines

          1. The board authorises (mandate and charter), positions (independence) and oversees (resources and quality) the function.
          2. The charter must set out the Purpose, commitment to the Standards, mandate, and position and reporting lines.
          3. Report functionally to the board, and administratively (ideally) to the CEO. Confirm independence at least annually.
          4. The board and senior management have essential conditions to fulfil. If they won’t, the CAE documents why and what it means for conformance.
          5. An external quality assessment happens at least every five years, with at least one active CIA on the team.

          Remember this

          Domain IV in five lines

          1. Understand governance, risk and control first, then set a strategy with a vision, objectives and initiatives.
          2. Base the plan on an at-least-annual documented risk assessment, keep it dynamic, and get board approval.
          3. Coordinate with other assurance providers. You can rely on their work, but you still own your conclusions.
          4. Manage money, people and technology, and tell the board promptly about shortfalls.
          5. Communicate accurately, objectively, clearly, concisely, constructively, completely and on time. Escalate unacceptable risk, but don’t resolve it yourself.

            ✦
            Remember this

            Domain V in five lines

            1. Plan: understand the activity, assess risks including fraud, set objectives and scope, agree the criteria, and get the work program approved.
            2. Gather evidence that is relevant, reliable and sufficient, enough that a competent peer would reach the same conclusion.
            3. A finding is the gap between criteria and condition. Explain its root cause, effect and significance.
            4. Conclude on effectiveness, and say so when things work.
            5. Issue a final communication with owners and dates, then follow up until actions are done or the risk is formally accepted.

            Applying the Standards in the Public Sector

            Audit in the agora

            The Standards apply to every function, but public-sector auditors work in a political environment where law may set the mandate, reporting lines, budget, and even require results to be presented in public meetings.

            Plate 38 · Interactive

            Five ways a public-sector CAE may be placed

            Some of these structures do not meet the independence requirements. The Standards suggest an audit committee of public members, independent of management, as a safeguard.

            Mandated focus

            Compliance with laws; efficiency, effectiveness and economy; safeguarding public resources and using them equitably; alignment of performance with strategic goals.

            Laws may override

            Mandates fixed by statute, public-disclosure rules, limits on private board sessions, mandated external auditors, and supreme audit institutions whose authority may supersede.

            Funding constraints

            Pay grades set by classification, approved software only, and peer programmes for EQAs when funds are short. The CAE can still advocate to the board.

            The UK Internal Audit Code of Practice

            The Chartered IIA's Code sets out 37 principles for internal audit in the UK and Ireland. For the first time, one Code covers the financial services, private and third sectors. It builds on the Global Internal Audit Standards and aims to raise practice above them.

            The Code does not replace the Standards; it should be applied alongside the IPPF and the GIAS. It is principles-based and applied proportionately to an organisation's nature, scope and complexity. Even so, every function is expected to engage with it, and external quality assessors will benchmark progress against it. It is aimed at board audit and risk committees, chief audit executives, non-executive directors, senior management and regulators. Public-sector functions should continue to follow the Public Sector Internal Audit Standards.

            “Now, more than ever, our profession needs to be bold and courageous.Internal Audit Code of Practice (2024) · Foreword · p. 2

            Remember this

            The UK Code in five lines

            1. From January 2025, one Code covers financial services, private and third sectors in the UK and Ireland.
            2. It adds to the GIAS and does not replace it. It is applied proportionately, and external assessors benchmark against it.
            3. Scope is unrestricted, with twelve named areas including culture, climate, financial crime and AI.
            4. The CAE sits at executive-committee level, reports primarily to the audit committee chair, and faces an annual independence discussion after seven years in post.
            5. Transparency: the CAE reports annually on how the Code was applied, and the audit committee reports on audit’s impact in the annual report.
